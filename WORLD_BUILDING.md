The six-phase structure gives the history a clear, causal progression:

**energy abundance → growth → exploration → desperation → reckless acceleration → ecological catastrophe → ideological fracture → isolation.**

The important thing is that every phase should feel like a reasonable reaction to the consequences of the previous one.

# I. Canonical Historical Chronology

For convenience, I would measure history from **Fungal Energy Discovery = Year 0**. The exact dates can move later.

## Phase 1 — Humanity Before the Crossing

### ~Year 0–40: The Energy Revolution

Humanity begins at roughly a late-industrial technological level. Population is growing, industrialization is putting increasing pressure on conventional energy supplies, and resource scarcity is becoming an important political concern.

Scientists discover a previously unremarkable fungal species that appears capable of extracting enormous amounts of energy from the planet.

They eventually learn to harvest that energy.

### Years 0–30: The Electromagnetic Revolution

Cheap fungal energy transforms human civilization.

Within approximately thirty years:

* electrical grids become ubiquitous;
* electric motors displace much combustion and steam machinery;
* metallurgy advances rapidly;
* enormous electromagnetic fields become practical;
* high-energy industrial processes become commonplace;
* magnetic transportation develops;
* large electromagnetic launchers and mass drivers become viable;
* radio and long-distance communications improve dramatically;
* electrically driven agriculture, irrigation and desalination expand.

However, **computation does not experience the same revolution**.

Human technology develops asymmetrically.

They can generate extraordinary amounts of power but still operate much of it with:

* mechanical controls;
* analog electronics;
* relays;
* vacuum tubes;
* human operators;
* crude electromechanical computers.

This becomes a defining characteristic of ancient human technology.

### The Success Problem

The fungal revolution works almost *too well*.

Food production increases.

Mortality falls.

Previously inhospitable regions become habitable.

Industrial output explodes.

The population expands rapidly.

For perhaps twenty years, humanity considers the mushroom its greatest discovery.

Then another reality becomes obvious:

> **Energy is abundant. Matter isn't.**

Humanity still needs metals, land, phosphorus, rare materials, biological resources and physical space.

The mushroom solved one limitation while allowing humanity to run much faster into another.

### Years 30–40: The Resonance Research Era

Governments massively increase funding for fungal research.

Scientists studying where fungal energy actually originates discover that the mycelial network interacts with something much deeper than conventional geothermal energy.

The planet itself appears to possess an underlying energetic structure.

More strangely, the fungus detects **other structures beyond the planet**.

Eventually the astonishing conclusion emerges:

**living worlds are somehow connected.**

Humanity learns to force two fungal systems into resonance.

The first portal opens.

---

### Technological State — Humans

**Extremely advanced relative to their starting point in:**

* electrical engineering;
* electromagnetics;
* power generation;
* industrial manufacturing;
* materials;
* electrochemistry;
* heavy transportation.

**Moderately advanced in:**

* radio;
* optics;
* medicine;
* mechanical engineering.

**Still comparatively immature in:**

* computation;
* miniaturized electronics;
* robotics;
* autonomous systems;
* artificial intelligence.

This technological imbalance should remain visible throughout ancient human civilization.

### Biological State

Humans remain biologically conventional.

The fungus is cultivated extensively but remains relatively close to its naturally occurring form.

### Cultural State

Human culture is extraordinarily optimistic.

The previous generation watched scarcity seemingly disappear.

A cultural belief develops that:

> **Human problems are problems waiting for engineering solutions.**

This isn't yet arrogance so much as experience.

Engineering really **has** repeatedly improved people's lives.

That belief becomes extremely important later.

---

# Phase 2 — The First Crossing

### ~Year 40–70: The Pathfinder Age

The first stable portal leads humanity to another inhabited world.

They encounter the **Giants**.

This is humanity's first confirmed encounter with another sapient species.

And critically:

### First contact goes well.

That should become almost mythologically important later.

The Giants are physically enormous—around five times human height—and naturally resistant to conditions that humans find extremely dangerous.

Their physiology tolerates:

* toxins;
* temperature variation;
* radiation;
* unfamiliar biological environments;
* atmospheric variations;
* fungal exposure.

I would also give Giants substantially longer lifespans—perhaps **150–250 years**—which allows the Giant found beneath the waterfall to have participated in the original expeditions and still be active during the later war.

### Human-Giant Cooperation

Humans possess extraordinarily powerful energy technology.

Giants possess knowledge humans don't.

I would make Giant technological development **different rather than simply superior**.

They may excel in:

* materials;
* geology;
* structural engineering;
* environmental survival;
* biological sciences;
* large-scale mechanics.

Humans excel in:

* electricity;
* electromagnetics;
* precision instrumentation;
* fungal energy manipulation.

The combination works remarkably well.

Joint expeditions begin.

This is where the protagonist's grapple harness originates.

Human explorers need to navigate:

* Giant architecture;
* massive geological formations;
* alien structures;
* extremely uneven terrain.

So the joint exploration corps develops powered traversal harnesses.

### The Golden Age of Exploration

For roughly thirty years, Humans and Giants explore nearby worlds.

They encounter several additional sapient species.

A couple develop limited partnerships with the Human-Giant expeditionary system.

But this is **not yet empire**.

Why?

Because moving matter through portals is extraordinarily expensive.

Sending:

* researchers;
* equipment;
* biological samples;
* rare minerals;

is possible.

Moving millions of tonnes of iron or food is not.

The portal network produces knowledge and valuable resources, but it cannot yet solve humanity's fundamental material problem.

---

### Technological State — Humans

New discoveries accelerate:

* fungal portal engineering;
* environmental equipment;
* advanced materials;
* electromagnetic traversal systems;
* portable fungal-energy devices;
* improved medicine;
* analog scientific instrumentation.

Computation improves, but remains secondary.

Ancient human equipment therefore still has a tactile character:

**switches, gauges, levers, optics, physical controls.**

### Technological State — Giants

Human energy technology rapidly expands Giant industrial capability.

Joint technology emerges.

Human devices become physically rugged through Giant engineering.

Giant equipment becomes dramatically more powerful through fungal energy.

### Biological State

Cross-world biological exposure begins.

Strict containment protocols exist.

Natural portal fungus remains slow-growing and relatively manageable.

Giants prove unusually resistant to both environmental and fungal effects.

### Cultural State — Humans

Explorers become heroes.

The Human-Giant partnership represents an optimistic vision:

> Different peoples can become greater together.

### Cultural State — Giants

Humans are often perceived as:

* physically fragile;
* extraordinarily inventive;
* impatient;
* intensely ambitious.

Giants generally possess a more cautious approach toward environmental intervention.

At this stage that difference is complementary rather than adversarial.

---

# Phase 3 — The Forced Bloom

### ~Year 70–85

Humanity's situation has improved.

But not fast enough.

Population continues growing.

Resource demand continues increasing.

Portal transportation remains the bottleneck.

The solution becomes obvious:

> **The portals need more energy.**

And the fungal networks need decades to mature naturally.

Human political leadership establishes massive research programs designed to accelerate fungal development.

This is the historical decision from which everything else follows.

## Humanity Mutates the Fungus

They select and manipulate it for:

* faster growth;
* greater energy extraction;
* stronger planetary resonance;
* denser mycelial networks;
* environmental adaptability;
* rapid reproduction.

I wouldn't necessarily make this modern-style precision genetic engineering.

It could combine:

* selective breeding;
* fungal grafting;
* chemically induced mutation;
* radiation;
* resonance-driven biological alteration;
* experimental gene manipulation learned through interworld biology.

That makes the process powerful but inherently less predictable.

And it works.

Portal throughput increases enormously.

A connection that once took years to mature may eventually mature in months.

Humanity suddenly begins importing resources on a scale capable of changing its economy.

### Then the fungus escapes.

Not primarily on the human homeworld, where massive containment infrastructure exists.

It escapes on frontier worlds.

There:

* monitoring is poor;
* ecosystems are unfamiliar;
* containment equipment is limited;
* biological interactions are unpredictable.

And the engineered fungus continues doing exactly what humanity designed it to do:

**adapt and grow.**

---

### Cultural State — Humanity

This is where the optimism of Phase 1 becomes dangerous.

Scientists warn that they do not fully understand the organism.

Political leaders respond:

> We don't have the luxury of waiting until we understand everything.

And they're not completely wrong.

Millions or billions depend upon greater resource imports.

### Cultural State — Giants

Giant concerns increase sharply.

Many Giant explorers consider the engineered fungus a violation of the principles under which exploration began.

But others defend humanity.

The Human-Giant relationship begins fracturing.

---

# Phase 4 — The Fracture

### ~Year 85–100

The engineered fungus begins transforming worlds.

But not consistently.

That's essential.

Different ecosystems produce radically different results.

On some worlds the fungus:

* destroys existing ecosystems.

Elsewhere:

* it forms symbioses.

Elsewhere:

* it alters neurological systems.

Some native populations become violently aggressive.

Others develop strange collective behavior.

Others gain beneficial adaptations.

Some fungi themselves begin showing increasingly complex behavior.

### The Giant Crisis

The Giants encounter devastated civilizations resulting directly from human fungal engineering.

Giant political leaders demand:

* an end to accelerated fungal deployment;
* suspension of expansion;
* independent investigations;
* assistance for affected worlds.

Human leadership refuses.

By now, interworld imports are essential to human civilization.

Stopping expansion doesn't mean economic inconvenience.

It means:

**massive human death.**

Human leaders therefore interpret Giant interference as an existential threat.

The narrative changes.

The former partners become:

> **traitors to the civilization they helped build.**

Evidence concerning fungal mutations is restricted.

Public explanations emphasize hostile native populations.

Human soldiers increasingly encounter aggressive infected species without understanding why they are aggressive.

The mythology becomes self-reinforcing:

> The other worlds are dangerous.

And eventually:

> Humanity must defend itself.

---

# Phase 5 — The Interworld War

### ~Year 100–125

The crisis fractures species **internally**, not simply along racial lines.

Humans fight humans.

Giants fight Giants.

Native civilizations choose different alliances.

Three ideological movements become the dominant forces.

---

# II. The Three Philosophies

## CONTINUITY

### Fundamental belief

> **Civilization creates obligations that cannot simply be abandoned.**

Billions of humans now depend upon the interworld economy.

Closing the network means knowingly permitting mass death.

Therefore Humanity must maintain access to the worlds.

### What Continuity gets right

The resource problem is real.

Destroying the network genuinely will kill enormous numbers of people.

Civilization cannot simply pretend the previous century never happened.

### Where Continuity fails

It gradually transforms:

> *We must save our people.*

into:

> *Anything done to save our people is justified.*

Native suffering becomes statistics.

Expansion becomes necessity.

Necessity becomes entitlement.

### Its great temptation

**Utilitarian arithmetic.**

Ten thousand aliens displaced to keep food flowing to ten million humans looks mathematically obvious.

Until you're one of the ten thousand.

---

# CONCORD

### Fundamental belief

> **Connection isn't the problem. Ignorance and domination are.**

The fungus is not universally destructive.

Some civilizations coexist with it.

Therefore the solution is understanding.

Humans, Giants, other civilizations and fungal life can potentially share the planetary network.

### What Concord gets right

They eventually prove that symbiosis is possible.

They produce some of the most peaceful and successful societies encountered by the protagonist.

### Where Concord fails

Knowledge itself becomes morally dangerous.

If a discovery could save billions, how many experimental subjects are acceptable?

How many failures?

How many transformed people?

Some Concord researchers begin believing that:

> **The potential future justifies the suffering necessary to reach it.**

### Its great temptation

**Progress for the sake of progress.**

---

# SEVERANCE

### Fundamental belief

> **Some systems are too dangerous to control and should not exist.**

Portal civilization inevitably produces:

* exploitation;
* fungal transfer;
* warfare;
* dependency.

The only permanent solution is separation.

### What Severance gets right

Every attempt to manage the network has produced another crisis.

A disconnected world cannot invade another disconnected world.

### Where Severance fails

Isolation has victims.

Destroying a portal can doom an entire civilization dependent upon it.

Severance can therefore become an ideology willing to kill millions today to prevent potentially greater suffering tomorrow.

### Its great temptation

**Purity through destruction.**

If connection causes suffering, then every connection becomes something to eliminate.

---

# III. The War's End

During approximately twenty-five years of conflict, the fungus radically transforms numerous worlds.

These become the worlds the protagonist later explores.

The war eventually reaches an exhausted equilibrium.

### Severance mostly succeeds.

They destroy or close the majority of the portal network.

Worlds become isolated.

### Concord fragments.

Small communities remain scattered throughout disconnected worlds.

Some have achieved extraordinary symbiosis.

Others continue unfinished experiments.

Some become almost religious custodians of fungal life.

### Continuity retreats.

Continuity retains the human homeworld and a **small lifeline network of resource worlds**.

This is important.

Otherwise human civilization could not survive another century.

Their interworld empire is gone.

What remains is essentially a civilization under permanent emergency conditions:

* rationing;
* declining infrastructure;
* controlled population;
* carefully maintained resource corridors.

They survive.

But barely.

---

# IV. The Giant Beneath the Waterfall

One of the Giants who participated in the original Pathfinder expeditions eventually joins the Severance-aligned refugees who become the protagonist's ancestors.

This gives his life a powerful historical arc.

He witnessed:

**First Contact → cooperation → exploration → fungal acceleration → war → collapse.**

He represents someone who once believed more strongly than almost anyone in interworld connection.

And eventually concludes that the connections must be closed.

When the refugees flee to the protagonist's world, he helps hide them.

Continuity scouts eventually discover the portal.

The Giant remains behind.

He kills the pursuing expedition and ensures the portal remains closed.

He dies in the cavern.

The equipment beneath him includes:

* the protagonist's harness;
* the fungal sample;
* the insignia of the original Pathfinder organization.

A century later, the protagonist finds him.

---

# Phase 6 — The Quiet Century

### ~Year 125–225

The protagonist's world remains disconnected for approximately 100 years.

This does require one specific cultural assumption to make the Renaissance-level society believable.

## The refugees deliberately dismantled their civilization.

They don't merely lose technology.

**They choose to abandon it.**

Advanced technology depends heavily upon fungal energy systems and specialized manufacturing infrastructure.

The refugees:

* destroy fungal power installations;
* destroy portal research;
* dismantle advanced manufacturing;
* prohibit large settlements;
* eliminate obvious electromagnetic signatures;
* scatter across the world.

This makes the regression much faster.

But I'd avoid making them suddenly scientifically ignorant.

Instead the protagonist's society should have interesting remnants.

They might possess unusually good:

* sanitation;
* metallurgy;
* mechanical engineering;
* medicine;
* agricultural practices;
* literacy.

Their **manufacturing technology looks Renaissance**, but some of their accumulated knowledge is strangely sophisticated.

That makes the starting world itself subtly unusual.

## The Hundred-Year Memory Problem

At only one century, the truth shouldn't disappear naturally.

So I would make **forgetting part of Severance policy**.

The first generation intentionally teaches its children simplified stories rather than the truth.

Why?

Because knowledge of the portals creates the possibility that someone will eventually reopen them.

The founders conclude:

> The safest civilization is one that doesn't know what it is hiding from.

Within four or five generations:

**Don't build detectable cities**

becomes:

> Giants in the clouds can see large settlements.

**Never activate fungal resonance**

becomes:

> Never plant the forbidden mushrooms.

**The Giant stayed behind to protect us**

eventually becomes:

> Giants descend from the heavens when men gather too closely.

This doesn't need to be perfect historical erasure.

There can be contradictory old stories, sealed records and suspicious ruins.

That gives you mysteries on the homeworld itself.

---

## The Starting World — The First Bloom

The opening world should be a functioning, isolated human settlement rather than a ruin.

The protagonist begins inside the life the Severance refugees built for themselves:

* a small village rather than a city;
* scattered homesteads rather than dense urban neighborhoods;
* practical crafts rather than advanced industry;
* inherited knowledge that is useful but deliberately incomplete;
* a culture that treats the old world as both dangerous and vaguely mythical.

The player should initially understand why this society chose isolation. It is safe, intimate and materially competent. It is also restrictive. The first portal is therefore not discovered by a professional expedition or a chosen hero. It is opened by two young people who are trying to postpone the moment when their lives become settled.

### The Village and the Choosing

The village sits in a sheltered valley near a waterfall, forest and broken highland terrain. The surrounding area has been explored enough for the villagers to know its reliable paths, hunting grounds and dangerous places, but not enough for anyone to have mapped the entire region.

The village is deliberately small. Outlying homes are normal because the founders prohibited large settlements, and because fields, grazing land and workshops need to be spread out. The protagonist's home is the farthest of these homesteads, beyond the main cluster of buildings. It is close enough to reach the village in a short walk, but far enough away that the protagonist has grown up with more independence than most villagers.

At the beginning of the story, both boys' parents are away on a trading journey. They have taken wares to another village some distance away and will be gone for several days. The trip is ordinary and necessary, but their absence leaves the protagonist's house without adult supervision at exactly the wrong time. It also means the boys can treat the home as a private place to investigate their discovery instead of immediately bringing it to the village leader.

At eighteen, every young person must choose a role that the community can depend on. The choice is presented as a coming-of-age freedom, but it is also an obligation. A village with too many hunters and no miller cannot survive, so the village leader and the elders exert pressure when a person's preference does not match the community's needs.

The protagonist is approximately seventeen, with one month remaining before the Choosing. He has not decided what he wants to become. He is capable in the outdoors, knows the local terrain unusually well and is happiest when he is somewhere he is not supposed to be. None of those qualities translate neatly into one of the village's recognized roles.

His closest friend has just turned eighteen.

The friend is curious, mechanically gifted and more intelligent than the village's simple title system has room to acknowledge. He has become the village smith, but the position also makes him its closest equivalent to an engineer. He repairs tools, maintains water and agricultural equipment, experiments with alloys and builds mechanisms that nobody else in the village fully understands.

He originally wanted to explore the continent. The village leader convinced him to stay by framing the choice as a moral responsibility: the village already has enough people who want to discover new places, but it needs someone who can keep the existing place alive. The friend accepts the role, partly because the argument is valid and partly because he feels guilty for wanting to leave.

That decision creates the emotional pressure between the two friends.

They are genuinely close. They have grown up together, explored the same ravines and forest paths, and know each other's habits almost as well as their own. They also compete constantly. They keep informal scores over who reaches a ledge first, who finds the better route and who is right about a dangerous shortcut. The protagonist is usually the bolder explorer. The friend is usually the one who understands how a thing works once they find it.

Each envies the other.

The protagonist sees his friend's new position as a surrender to the village. The friend sees the protagonist's uncertainty as a luxury made possible by other people's work. Neither says this plainly, because their rivalry is also how they express affection.

### The Forbidden Mushroom

Mushrooms are treated as poisonous and socially dangerous throughout the village. Children are taught never to touch unfamiliar growth, and adults burn or isolate any large or unusually colored mushroom they find. The warning is not entirely superstition: ordinary fungal blooms can cause severe illness, and the community has inherited fragments of the founders' fear without understanding its technological origin.

The village has no concept of engineered portal fungus. It only has a prohibition:

> **Do not bring strange mushrooms home.**

The specimen the friends find is dormant and sealed inside an old expedition sack. It looks like a dense, dry, dark growth rather than a living network. It does not resemble the small mushrooms that cause ordinary sickness, which is part of why the friend is fascinated by it. The protagonist is less impressed. He sees a forbidden biological object beside a dead Giant and argues that both should be left where they are.

The specimen is in fact a surviving culture of the old portal fungus. It has been inactive for a century, but it still contains the resonance patterns needed to reconnect with an ancient anchor.

### The Cavern Discovery

One month before the protagonist's birthday, the two friends decide to push beyond the furthest routes they have explored together. The trip is partly an adventure and partly an argument about the future. The friend wants to prove that becoming the village smith has not made him ordinary. The protagonist wants one more expedition before the Choosing can close off his options.

They reach a waterfall and find a narrow route behind it. The passage is not marked on any village map. They begin competing over who can cross a natural shelf first, and the weakened ground gives way beneath them. They fall into a cavern below the waterfall.

The fall is frightening but survivable. More importantly, it leaves them inside a sealed chamber that no living villager has seen.

There they discover the Giant.

The Giant is dead, preserved beneath the cavern's cold spray and mineral deposits. This is not a monster encounter. It is the first physical evidence that the stories about Giants contain a distorted memory of a real people. The Giant's body is large enough to make the friends feel like children in the chamber, but its equipment is clearly manufactured rather than ceremonial.

Beside the body is an expedition pack. Inside they find:

* a folded traversal body suit and harness;
* the dormant fungal specimen;
* unfamiliar tools and fittings;
* the insignia of the original Pathfinder organization.

The friends do not know the words *harness*, *Pathfinder* or *portal*. They interpret the suit as some kind of protective body armor, and the insignia as evidence that the Giant belonged to an organized people rather than being a supernatural creature.

The protagonist's first instinct is to leave everything in the cavern. The dead should be left undisturbed, and taking the mushroom violates the strongest rule they have been taught. The friend argues that they may never find the chamber again. He also points out that if they return to the village with nothing but a story, nobody will believe them.

The argument is strengthened by their rivalry. The friend does not want to return from the greatest discovery of their lives empty-handed, especially now that he has supposedly chosen a life of staying put. Eventually he convinces the protagonist to take the pack.

### Two Secrets

The friends decide not to return to the village with the discovery. The protagonist's parents are away, the friend's parents are away and the nearest adults are far enough away that the boys can investigate without being interrupted. They go directly to the protagonist's house.

They hide the fungal specimen beside the house. They do not understand containment, so they loosely bury the sack under soil and leaves rather than opening it or destroying it. They know mushrooms are considered toxic, but the discovery is too interesting to throw away. Their decision should feel like the kind of foolish compromise made by intelligent teenagers: cautious enough to believe they are being responsible, reckless enough to create a catastrophe.

They bring the body suit inside and take it to the protagonist's bedroom. The friend has only the portable tools he carries for field repairs and whatever household tools he can find, but his curiosity is stronger than the limitations of the workspace.

He spends the evening examining the suit. He tests clasps, flexible plates, lenses, cables and unfamiliar contact points. The suit is too sophisticated to be armor, but too tactile and mechanical to resemble anything from the village. It has no obvious source of power and no visible weapon.

The protagonist eventually puts it on so they can test how it moves on a human body. It fits more naturally than either of them expects. The friend forms a theory about how to activate it, probably by closing a circuit through one of the hand controls or pulling a lever near the wrist. He tries the sequence.

Nothing happens.

They leave the suit on the protagonist because removing it risks damaging the unfamiliar fastenings, and because they are exhausted from the expedition. They fall asleep in the bedroom with the protagonist still wearing the harness. The friend's field tools, the sack's empty packing material and the unfamiliar suit are all gathered around them, turning the bedroom into a secret workshop for one night.

This is the last moment when the discovery still feels like a secret they can put back.

### The First Bloom

In the early morning, the two boys wake in the bedroom as the house shakes. At first they think the cavern may have collapsed or that the earthquake is a local aftershock. Then the floor tilts beneath them and a deep cracking sound comes from the main part of the house.

They get outside and see something rising above the trees. A colossal mushroom is growing from the ground beneath the protagonist's home. Its stalk has seized the central part of the house—the kitchen, living room and adjoining common spaces—and is lifting it into the sky. The bedroom where the boys slept is left behind at the edge of the structure, cracked but still standing. The rest of the home is being carried upward in pieces.

The arrangement should make the bloom immediately personal and visually legible: the boys are safe for the moment because their bedroom has been spared, while the rooms that represent family life are disappearing above them. The kitchen hearth, living-room table, shelves and roof beams become parts of the rising route.

The village reacts with panic. The old stories say that a large mushroom brings sickness, but no story describes one that grows faster than a person can run. Adults begin shouting for people to move away from the homestead. The village leader tries to organize an evacuation while others reach for fire, axes and water.

The protagonist does not wait for instructions. He runs toward the bloom because the main part of his home is inside it, still wearing the body suit.

The friend follows from behind.

The suit remains inert during the first part of the run. As the protagonist approaches the mushroom, small lines and fittings along the harness begin to emit a weak light. The friend recognizes that the suit is responding to the bloom and shouts for him to activate it.

This time the activation works.

The first grapple fires from the harness and catches on the mushroom's living surface. The protagonist is pulled off the ground before he understands what has happened. He nearly loses his grip, slams into the stalk and discovers that the suit is not a climbing device in the ordinary sense. It is a traversal system that converts a target into a route.

The friend is frightened by how quickly the protagonist is being pulled upward. He also recognizes the impossible opportunity in front of them. The mushroom is responding to the old equipment, the house is being carried toward its upper growth and the path cannot remain stable forever.

Their old pattern returns under pressure: the friend is the one who understands the mechanism, and the protagonist is the one willing to use it.

The friend tells the protagonist to go. He says he will catch up as quickly as he can, whether by finding another route, retrieving tools or helping the villagers below. The promise is partly reassurance and partly a challenge. Neither friend is ready to admit that the first crossing may separate them.

### The House in the Sky

The ascent becomes the opening traversal sequence.

The protagonist grapples from fungal ridges to broken beams, wall-runs along sections of his own house and uses familiar objects in unfamiliar positions. A kitchen window becomes a platform. A roof becomes a vertical wall. The family table, shelves and hearth appear at impossible heights along the stalk, allowing the player to see that the bloom is not merely destroying the house; it is pulling the communal heart of the home into itself.

The largest section of the house rests at the top of the mushroom. It is the main living and kitchen area, torn upward almost intact. Inside it, the protagonist finds a space that is larger than the room should be. The walls have opened around a circular structure of fungal tissue, metal and pale light. The dormant anchor beneath the homestead has been reconnected to the specimen, and the old portal system has grown a new path around the house.

There is a portal inside.

The protagonist has no historical framework for what he is seeing. To him it is a doorway made from the forbidden thing his village fears, connected to the dead Giant's impossible equipment. The friend is still climbing below, promising to catch up. The village is below him. His home is above him. The only recognizable route leads through the light.

The protagonist crosses first, believing the friend will follow immediately. The friend reaches the top moments later, just in time to see the protagonist on the other side. For a brief interval they can still see and hear each other through the portal, but the opening begins to contract as the bloom consumes the last of its resonance. Neither can cross back in time.

The starting world ends with the portal disappearing between them—not with the discovery of the doorway, but with the first connection becoming the first separation.

### Why the Bloom Happens

The recommended explanation is that the opening is caused by three pieces of the old system meeting again:

1. **The fungal specimen** is a dormant portal culture carried by the Giant.
2. **The harness** is a Pathfinder traversal and resonance device. The friend's attempted activation places it into a low-power standby state even though it does not yet produce a grapple.
3. **The protagonist's homestead** was built over a buried, inert portal anchor. The founders dismantled the active machinery, but one anchor was too dangerous to remove and was hidden beneath an outlying home. Its purpose was forgotten over generations.

The friend's tinkering wakes the harness. The buried specimen responds through the anchor during the night. At dawn, the fungus blooms upward, using the house and the old anchor as a physical and energetic scaffold. Because the anchor sits beneath the central foundation, the mushroom takes the kitchen and living room first while leaving the bedroom wing behind. The suit only fully activates when it comes close enough to the living growth to complete the resonance.

This keeps the event from feeling random while preserving the characters' point of view: they caused it, but they did not understand what they were activating.

### What the Starting World Establishes

The opening world should establish the game's central tensions on a personal scale:

* **Duty versus freedom:** the friend stays to serve the village while the protagonist is about to choose whether to stay at all.
* **Curiosity versus caution:** the friend keeps touching things that should have been left alone, while the protagonist recognizes the danger but is pulled into it anyway.
* **Connection versus isolation:** the village's rules protected it for a century, but those same rules left its people unable to understand the technology now changing their lives.
* **History versus myth:** the Giant is real, the Pathfinder insignia is real and the old portal still works, even though the village's explanation for all three has become legend.
* **Friendship versus rivalry:** the first portal opens because the friends act together, but the ascent begins with one friend going ahead and the other promising to follow.

The mushroom should not be presented as simply evil. It is dangerous, invasive and capable of catastrophe, but it is also the surviving mechanism that reconnects the protagonist to the wider history of the worlds. The starting world should leave the player with the first version of the question that later worlds complicate:

> **Is a dangerous connection still worth making if isolation is the only alternative you have ever known?**

### Details to Keep Flexible for Now

Several choices can remain open while the later worlds are developed:

* the names of the protagonist, friend, village and village leader;
* what wares the parents are carrying and which distant village they are visiting;
* whether the founders deliberately placed a homestead over the inert anchor or whether later settlers did so unknowingly;
* whether the Giant carried the specimen as an emergency means of reopening the old route or simply as Pathfinder research that survived the war;
* the exact form of the friend's beacon and the point in the Human Homeworld arc where the reunion occurs.

The emotional sequence should remain fixed even if those surface details change:

> **A final adventure before adulthood → a forbidden discovery → a secret shared between friends → an accidental bloom → a house lifted into the sky → a first step through history → the portal closes between them.**

## The Friend's Parallel Arc — The Beacon

The protagonist's journey should not begin when the friend disappears. The friend becomes the second half of the opening story the moment the portal closes.

The protagonist crosses into the unknown believing that the friend will follow immediately. The friend remains in the starting world believing that the protagonist is still reachable. Both spend the next several months acting on the same promise:

> **Find a way back to each other.**

Their methods eventually lead them toward opposite sides of the game's central conflict.

### The Separation

The protagonist enters the portal first. The friend reaches the top of the mushroom only moments later, still expecting to step through behind him.

For a brief time, the portal remains open as a shrinking window. The protagonist turns around and sees his friend on the other side. The friend sees the alien world behind the protagonist. Both try to cross or reach through, but the portal is already contracting.

The protagonist may fire the grapple toward the friend, but the harness cannot attach across the unstable boundary. The friend reaches for him, but the portal is no longer a doorway that can support another crossing. It is a connection consuming the last of its available resonance.

They watch each other in horror as the opening closes.

This should not feel like the protagonist knowingly chooses exploration over his friend. He enters because the friend tells him to go, because both believe the friend will follow and because neither understands that the bloom can only sustain one crossing. The friend is not too late because he hesitates; he is too late because the rules of the old technology are unknown to both of them.

The disappearance gives the friend a clear wound and a clear objective. The protagonist is not merely lost in another world. He is alive, visible and suddenly unreachable.

### The Eradication

The village arrives shortly afterward.

The villagers see a towering toxic mushroom wrapped around the protagonist's home. They do not understand that the portal has already closed, and they have no reason to believe the protagonist survived. To them, the bloom is an uncontrolled outbreak and a direct threat to the settlement.

They decide to eradicate it.

The response should be frightened and practical rather than cruel. The village leader orders the growth burned, cut and isolated before it can spread. The villagers dismantle the parts of the house that remain caught in the stalk and destroy the visible fungal tissue.

The friend knows that destroying the bloom may destroy the only evidence of where the protagonist went. He cannot openly stop the village without revealing that he helped cause the event and hid the specimen. Instead, he secretly salvages a small collection of pieces before the main growth is destroyed.

This is the second separation. The first takes the protagonist away. The second threatens to remove the only remaining path to him.

### Studying Without Planting

The friend keeps the fragments hidden and studies them over the following months. He does not plant them.

That distinction should remain central to his character. He has seen what an uncontrolled bloom can do to a home and a village. He understands that simply growing another mushroom would be reckless, so he keeps the samples sealed in containers and observes how they respond to heat, vibration, light, metal and carefully controlled electrical current.

His new role as the village smith gives him access to tools, metalworking equipment and a reason to work late without immediately attracting suspicion. The same position that once felt like a compromise becomes the foundation of his research.

He gradually discovers that the samples are not only alive. They are receiving and repeating patterns. Some patterns cause the tissue to become active. Others produce a weak glow or a change in growth direction. The friend does not yet understand the purpose, but he realizes that the fungus is communicating with something beyond the village.

This period should run in parallel with the protagonist's early exploration. While the protagonist learns to use the harness in unfamiliar worlds, the friend learns to read the biological system that powers the old network.

### The Beacon

The friend eventually builds a device that does not grow a portal but signals one.

Using the surviving fungal fragments, old metal from the bloom and his own mechanical experiments, he creates a crude resonance beacon. It is an analog machine of coils, plates, tuned components and living material held in strict containment.

The first versions fail. Some go dark. Some produce only heat. One may briefly cause a small fungal tendril to seek a direction before going inert. The friend keeps refining the device rather than planting the samples, because he now understands that the signal matters more than the growth.

Over months, the beacon begins broadcasting a recognizable Pathfinder pattern.

It is not a message in language. It is a location signal—a lighthouse built from the remains of the first bloom. The friend is telling the surviving human network:

> **This world is still here. Something from the old system is still alive.**

The signal is weak and intermittent, which gives the human Continuity network time to detect it, interpret it and prepare a response. The reconnection is therefore deliberate and technically difficult, not an immediate consequence of the protagonist's portal.

### The Human Expedition

The original human civilization eventually triggers a portal to the friend's world.

The expedition arrives expecting to find a lost settlement, an old portal anchor or a surviving human colony. Instead, they find an isolated village, a damaged bloom and the young smith who created the signal.

The friend goes with them when they offer access to human archives, advanced equipment and the surviving portal infrastructure. He is not abandoning the village. He believes that reaching the human world is the only realistic way to find the protagonist.

He becomes the expedition's local technical expert. He can explain what happened at the bloom, how the beacon works and what little he knows about the harness. The humans can provide the knowledge he lacks, while he provides the living specimen and the coordinates they could not have recovered alone.

The friend continues working with human scientists to study the fungal system and identify the route the protagonist took. His research is driven by a personal goal, but it also gives the human civilization something it desperately needs: a way to restore reliable access to the wider network.

### Why He Supports the Humans

The friend arrives in the human world expecting to find answers. He also finds a civilization in permanent resource conflict.

The human homeworld is beautiful and populated by ordinary people, but its cities, hospitals and agricultural systems are under constant pressure. Shortages lead to political conflict and sometimes violence. The friend sees that the resource crisis is not an abstract justification invented by leaders. It is harming people he can meet.

This changes his position.

He does not initially support uncontrolled expansion. His preferred solution is a limited and regulated reconnection:

* controlled portals;
* strict biological containment;
* small exploratory teams;
* no automatic extraction rights;
* research before large-scale transfer.

But he believes connection must remain possible. He has experienced isolation as the loss of his closest friend, and he now sees the human world experiencing isolation as hunger, instability and conflict.

His argument becomes a practical form of Continuity:

> **The portals are dangerous, but closing them permanently leaves people with no way to solve the crisis that is already killing them.**

The human leaders use his qualifications and personal story to support a broader policy than he intended. His careful statements about controlled reconnection become public claims that portal travel is safe again.

They also use the protagonist as erroneous proof. A living traveler from a supposedly lost world is presented as evidence that the old dangers have been overcome. The truth is narrower: one person survived one unusual crossing through one unstable bloom. That does not prove that every world is safe, that every fungal strain is controllable or that extraction can begin again.

The friend understands some of these limitations, but his need for the project to succeed makes him underestimate how quickly safeguards can become obstacles in the eyes of people facing immediate shortages.

### The Late Reunion

By the time the protagonist reaches the Human Homeworld, the original purpose of his journey has changed.

At the beginning, he wants to find his friend and return home. After traveling through the other worlds, he learns what the portal network did to civilizations and sees how easily a necessary connection becomes entitlement, extraction and war. His goal becomes preventing history from repeating, even if that means accepting that he may never see his friend again.

The friend, meanwhile, has spent those same months doing everything he can to find the protagonist. He has created the beacon, crossed into the human world and helped rebuild the path that eventually brings the protagonist there.

The reunion should begin with genuine relief. Neither expected the other to survive. For a moment, the separation is undone.

Then the contradiction becomes unavoidable.

The friend is now part of the human reconnection effort. The protagonist has arrived as someone increasingly suspicious of that effort. The friend believes he has fulfilled the promise to find him. The protagonist sees that the path created to find one person is becoming a justification for opening every world again.

Their conflict is not a simple betrayal. Both have remained loyal to the promise they made at the first portal, but they have spent months learning different lessons from the separation.

The friend believes:

> **Connection is worth the danger because people cannot survive isolation forever.**

The protagonist believes:

> **Connection cannot be justified merely because people are afraid of what isolation costs.**

The friend becomes the personal face of Continuity for the protagonist. He is not a distant administrator or an abstract political faction. He is the person the protagonist crossed worlds to find.

The original goal has not truly become irrelevant. The protagonist has buried it beneath a larger responsibility. The reunion forces him to confront the fact that his personal hope and the political crisis are still connected.

### Narrative Function

The two arcs should remain different in form:

* the protagonist moves outward through worlds and learns the consequences of the old network;
* the friend moves inward toward the surviving human civilization and learns why the network was considered necessary;
* the protagonist becomes more cautious about reconnection;
* the friend becomes more committed to making reconnection work;
* the protagonist is separated from home by a portal that closes;
* the friend helps reopen the network because he refuses to accept that separation as permanent.

Their reunion is therefore the moment when the game's personal story and political story become the same story.

The friend should not be treated as a hidden villain. His position is strongest when he is partly right. Controlled connection may genuinely be necessary to prevent human collapse. The tragedy is that his success gives leaders the confidence to expand faster than the safeguards can support.

The friend should leave the protagonist—and the player—with the uncomfortable version of the Continuity argument:

> **You learned what connection did to the worlds. I learned what the absence of connection did to us.**

# V. World Stories

I would design the journey so that each world doesn't simply teach the player "the lore."

Each should force the player to **live through a smaller version of the same philosophical problem**.

The global conflict should always be reflected through a handful of people.

---

# World 1 — The Last Garden

### Dominant philosophy: Severance

This could be the glowing wasteland immediately beyond the protagonist's first portal.

Most native life has been transformed.

The protagonist encounters three survivors:

**The Keeper** — an elderly woman who burns fungal growth continuously and has protected one surviving settlement for decades.

**Her Son** — partially infected, but apparently still himself.

**The Hunter** — another survivor who believes every infected organism must be destroyed immediately.

The son begins showing strange behavior.

But he also develops an ability to predict fungal blooms and protects the settlement.

The Keeper increasingly depends upon him.

The Hunter sees only the inevitable outcome.

### The personal conflict

Is the son:

* sick;
* adapting;
* becoming something else;
* or all three?

There should be no simple answer.

### Thesis

> **Removing danger and destroying what is dangerous aren't necessarily the same thing.**

This world introduces the player to Severance logic before they even know Severance existed.

---

# World 2 — The Hero of the Wall

### Dominant philosophy: Continuity

A fortified civilization has survived decades of attacks from aggressive fungal-mutated creatures.

Its legendary military commander has repeatedly saved the settlement.

The player gradually discovers that **the commander is infected**.

Fungal growth is spreading through his nervous system.

It's partly responsible for his extraordinary combat ability.

It's also slowly affecting his judgment.

The central characters might be:

**The Commander** — genuinely heroic.

**His Daughter / Lieutenant** — knows something is wrong but fears losing the only person capable of protecting the city.

**A Captured Infected Native** — appears monstrous initially but begins demonstrating coherent intelligence.

### The tragedy

The Commander continues defending his people.

Every heroic act exposes him further.

Eventually:

> The quality making him their greatest protector is also destroying the person he was.

### Thesis

> **When does protecting something become justification for refusing to let go?**

This mirrors Continuity later.

---

# World 3 — The Severed World

### Dominant philosophy: The cost of Severance

This world once depended heavily upon another planet.

When Severance closed its portal, access to critical medicine or ecological technology disappeared.

Millions died.

Two surviving characters represent opposing memories.

**The Archivist** lost most of her family and considers Severance mass murderers.

**The Farmer** survived because cutting the portal also stopped an incoming fungal catastrophe.

They both remember the same historical event.

One says:

> They abandoned us.

The other says:

> They saved whoever could still be saved.

Neither is lying.

### Thesis

> **The same decision can simultaneously be salvation and catastrophe.**

---

# World 4 — The Giant's Memory

### Dominant philosophy: Collapse of idealism

The protagonist reaches a surviving Giant settlement.

Here they meet an elderly Giant who participated in the Pathfinder Age.

Perhaps this individual personally knew the Giant beneath the waterfall.

Initially the elder refuses to discuss humans.

A younger Giant sees humanity exclusively as the civilization that destroyed worlds.

The elder remembers something the younger generation doesn't:

**when Humans and Giants were friends.**

Through this relationship, the protagonist learns about:

* First Contact;
* the Pathfinder expeditions;
* the original exploration corps;
* the mysterious Giant hero.

Eventually the elder recognizes the insignia on the protagonist's harness.

Not necessarily the harness itself.

Just the symbol.

The protagonist still doesn't know exactly what this means.

### Thesis

> **History remembers what people became more easily than what they once were.**

---

# World 5 — The Joined

### Dominant philosophy: Concord

This world appears at first to be the closest thing in the game to a utopia.

The fungus exists everywhere.

The inhabitants are healthy.

Violence is rare.

Disease is easily treated.

People can share emotion and partial memories through their fungal networks.

Then the protagonist meets:

**The Dissenter** — someone who wants their fungal connection removed.

**The Caretaker** — the person responsible for maintaining the network.

**The Dissenter's Partner** — someone terrified that disconnection will fundamentally change the person they love.

The society allows disconnection in theory.

But doing so removes many abilities required to participate in everyday life.

### The question

If everyone voluntarily participates in a system because life outside it is extremely difficult...

**how voluntary is participation?**

And yet the society genuinely works.

### Thesis

> **Harmony has costs just as conflict does.**

This should be one of the strongest arguments that Concord might actually be right.

---

# World 6 — The Gentle Mind

### Dominant philosophy: Concord corrupted by its own curiosity

This incorporates your idea about a sentient fungus.

A failed Concord research site produced something unprecedented:

**a fungal organism with clear individual consciousness.**

And it is peaceful.

Perhaps even childlike.

The surviving researchers disagree about what to do.

**The Researcher** desperately wants to study it because understanding fungal consciousness could change everything.

**The Guardian** helped create it and now believes experimentation must stop because it is a person.

**The Fungal Being** simply wants not to be hurt.

The researcher isn't necessarily cruel.

Their argument is terrifying precisely because it makes sense:

> If studying one conscious fungal organism could prevent billions of deaths, are we really justified in refusing?

### Thesis

> **The desire to understand something does not grant ownership over it.**

This is Concord's darkest temptation.

---

# World 7 — The Lifeline

### Dominant philosophy: Continuity

This world contains one of Continuity's few surviving resource operations.

The protagonist initially sees:

* environmental destruction;
* military occupation;
* displaced natives;
* brutal extraction.

It appears straightforward.

Then they meet the people responsible.

**The Human Administrator** receives weekly quotas from the homeworld.

Missing those quotas means hospitals lose power or food production declines.

**The Native Leader** wants the extraction stopped but knows that attacking the facility will cause massive civilian casualties elsewhere.

**A Human Worker** has never seen the human homeworld and was born on this planet.

Now the moral arithmetic becomes uncomfortable.

Stopping the mine helps the people standing in front of you.

Continuing it helps millions you've never met.

### Thesis

> **Distance makes exploitation easier to justify—and sacrifice harder to understand.**

This prepares the player for the human homeworld.

---

# VI. Then the Human Homeworld

This should be one of the game's largest reversals.

After everything the protagonist has learned, they finally reach the civilization responsible for much of the catastrophe.

And it is...

**beautiful.**

Children play.

Universities operate.

Hospitals work.

People make art.

Families live ordinary lives.

They aren't monsters.

Most citizens know almost nothing about what happened.

Then the protagonist appears.

A human from a world thought lost.

With functioning portal technology.

By this point, the friend has already been living in the human world for months. His beacon brought the starting world back to the attention of the surviving human network, and he has worked with human scientists to understand the fungal system and search for the protagonist's route.

He has become a respected technical voice in the reconnection effort. He argues for controlled portals and careful study, but his qualifications and personal story are used to support a much broader policy than he intended.

The protagonist becomes erroneous proof that the age of isolation can end safely. His survival demonstrates that one person crossed one unstable portal. Continuity leaders present it as evidence that the old dangers have been overcome.

The friend is on the human side of the argument when the protagonist arrives. He believes the portals must remain available because the human homeworld is locked in constant conflict over dwindling resources. The reunion therefore becomes both a personal recovery and an ideological confrontation.

The political slogan practically writes itself:

> **THE WORLDS ARE OPEN AGAIN.**

And because the protagonist has traveled through numerous apparently viable worlds, Continuity leaders see an opportunity to finally solve the century-long resource crisis.

Exploration restarts.

Then extraction.

Then resistance.

Then casualties.

Then propaganda.

And eventually the protagonist recognizes exactly what is happening:

**the history they've spent the entire game uncovering is beginning again around them.**

Some of the leaders who restarted it realize the same thing and attempt to stop it.

But now they discover the final expression of your theme:

> **People can create systems that eventually become larger than their intentions.**

They built the propaganda.

They promised prosperity.

They told people the other worlds would save them.

They celebrated the explorers.

Now saying *stop* doesn't undo any of that.

# The Three-Act Story and Mission Structure

The story should be built as a three-act progression. Each act asks a larger version of the same question about connection:

* **Act 1 — The Unstable Door:** Can the protagonist understand the worlds and find his friend?
* **Act 2 — The Controlled Door:** Can the portal network be used responsibly?
* **Act 3 — The Door That Must Be Chosen:** What should be done when control fails?

The acts should share one primary story path. Major decisions should change relationships, survivors, enemy states, mission conditions and the meaning of the ending rather than creating three entirely separate campaigns.

## Act 1 — The Unstable Door

The starting-world story and the friend's parallel beacon arc form the first act.

The protagonist begins as a seventeen-year-old who is one month away from the village's required role Choosing. He discovers the Giant, the fungal specimen and the traversal harness with his best friend, accidentally activates the first bloom and crosses through the portal alone.

### Unstable portal travel

Natural or accidentally regenerated portals are not reliable doorways. They can open long enough for a person or a small amount of matter to pass through, but the connection destabilizes after matter crosses.

The protagonist cannot simply step back through the same portal. The passage loses its phase lock, collapses or redirects its remaining energy into another fungal growth. The harness helps him identify and activate available connections, but it does not stabilize the portal behind him.

This gives the protagonist's Act 1 world hopping a clear purpose. He is moving forward because every crossing removes the route behind him. He is trying to understand the portal system and find a way back to his friend, while each new world reveals another part of the history of fungal energy, the Giants and the war.

The worlds he visits should also give him the first encounters with:

* corrupted Giants who appear to confirm the human belief that Giants are dangerous;
* wary but uncorrupted Giants who can be avoided, helped or cautiously approached by a lone human;
* communities that survived earlier fungal releases;
* the consequences of Severance, Continuity and Concord before the protagonist knows those philosophies by name.

A lone human is less threatening than an organized expedition. The protagonist can often get around Giant groups by avoiding them, helping an individual or proving that he is not establishing an outpost. This becomes much more difficult in Act 2 when humans arrive with equipment, quotas and a visible military or industrial presence.

### The friend's Act 1 path

After watching the protagonist disappear, the friend remains behind. The village attempts to eradicate the bloom, and he secretly preserves small samples without planting them. He studies their reactions and eventually builds a contained resonance beacon.

The beacon does not grow a portal. It broadcasts a recognizable Pathfinder signal that allows the surviving human network to detect the starting world. The original human civilization opens a portal to the friend's world, and he travels with the expedition to the Human Homeworld.

There he learns how to combine the fungal system with surviving human electromagnetic stabilization technology. The friend believes that controlled portals can allow people to reconnect without repeating the uncontrolled spread that destroyed the old worlds.

### Act 1 endpoint

The protagonist reaches the Human Homeworld and reunites with the friend.

The protagonist has achieved his original personal goal, but the journey has changed it. He no longer wants only to return home. He now understands that his friend is connected to a much larger question: whether the worlds should be connected again at all.

## Portal Stability Progression

The three acts should also represent three stages of portal technology.

### Natural portals

Natural or uncontrolled fungal portals:

* are temporary;
* destabilize after matter crosses;
* cannot reliably support return travel;
* may connect to unexpected destinations;
* can spread fungal growth into the receiving world;
* are navigated through the harness but not properly controlled by it.

These are the portals the protagonist encounters during Act 1.

### Human-stabilized portals

Human technology creates a more reliable connection through paired anchors, electromagnetic regulators, stored energy and careful calibration.

These portals can support repeated travel, but only if:

* both sides have compatible anchors;
* the stabilizer is correctly calibrated;
* the portal remains within its safe matter and time limits;
* the fungal culture is contained;
* the expedition follows the return procedure.

These are the portals used by the controlled expeditions in Act 2.

### Concord-assisted portals

The Concord world contributes a biological buffer that can help regulate fungal resonance. It cannot create portals by itself, but it can make a stabilized network more adaptive and potentially safer for multiple species.

This requires the protagonists to combine:

* human electromagnetic engineering;
* Giant geological and structural knowledge;
* Concord fungal symbiosis;
* biological information from other intelligent species.

This combined system is the basis of the Act 3 stabilization effort and the possible Concord ending.

## Act 2 — The Controlled Door

### Reconnection and the expedition

After the reunion, the friend convinces the protagonist to join the new human expedition program.

The expedition is presented as a controlled alternative to the reckless expansion of the past. The protagonist becomes a field and traversal specialist. The friend is responsible for portal calibration, stabilization and interpreting the fungal system.

The early missions should succeed. The expeditioners restore supply routes, recover materials, provide aid and rescue people without immediately causing a catastrophe. The player should understand why ordinary humans support the program.

The friend can honestly argue:

> **We finally have a chance to do this correctly.**

### The expedition mission loop

Each expedition should have at least two goals:

1. A practical objective such as rescuing people, recovering resources or stabilizing an anchor.
2. A local relationship or research objective involving the people and fungal life of that world.

The missions should produce different kinds of progress:

* **Portal materials** such as conductors, anchors and field components improve future stabilization.
* **Biological samples** reveal how different fungal strains interact with different hosts.
* **Collaboration** gives the protagonists the cultural knowledge, trust and consent needed to apply that research safely.

The expedition can sometimes obtain one form of progress without the others. Taking a sample by force may improve the raw research while damaging collaboration. Helping a community may improve trust without producing a useful biological sample. The strongest outcomes require both.

### The gradual compromise

The human leadership's greed should appear as a series of successful compromises:

* portal windows are shortened to increase the number of expeditions;
* quotas are raised after earlier missions exceed expectations;
* quarantine periods are reduced;
* native warnings are dismissed because previous warnings proved overly cautious;
* stabilizers are reused beyond their recommended maintenance limits;
* richer resource zones are opened before the full safety survey is complete.

None of these decisions needs to fail immediately. Their success teaches the characters and the player that the restrictions may be excessive.

The final catastrophe should be the result of a chain rather than one obviously evil action:

> **Resource pressure → increased quotas → weakened safeguards → normalized risk → personal emergency → one final shortcut.**

### Giants during Act 2

The expedition encounters Giants throughout the controlled-expedition phase.

Some Giants are corrupted by the fungus and become enemies. Some are uncorrupted survivor groups who are frightened, defensive or openly hostile toward human equipment. All Giants should be wary of humans, even when they are willing to cooperate.

The official human civilization has taught that Giants are evil. Corrupted Giants make that story appear credible at first. Survivor groups challenge it.

Possible Giant missions include:

* fighting a corrupted Giant group that is attacking an expedition route;
* rescuing an injured survivor group that refuses to let humans approach;
* supplying a Giant community while abandoning an extraction objective;
* helping a Giant group escape a fungal bloom;
* deciding whether a corrupted Giant can be saved or must be fought.

The protagonist can often approach Giant groups more successfully when he is alone. A full human expedition looks much more like an invasion force. The team's actions determine whether a survivor group accepts aid, remains cautious or refuses all further contact.

At least one Giant group should receive meaningful aid during the story. This is a required demonstration that Humans and Giants can work together again. Other groups can have variable outcomes based on the player's decisions.

A saved Giant group may later provide:

* a safe route through Giant territory;
* knowledge about an old portal anchor;
* structural or geological expertise;
* a biological sample or symbiosis record;
* a cautious alliance during the Act 3 war.

A group that is frightened or mistreated may refuse this assistance. Cooperation should be earned and remain conditional rather than turning into instant friendship.

### The Resource World

The resource-rich hostile world should be a place the protagonist has already visited during Act 1.

During the earlier visit, he meets a refugee community that has already rebuilt its life there after escaping a fungal release on another world. The protagonist knows the world as a home, not as an empty resource site. The unstable portal forces him to leave before he can remain or promise meaningful help.

In Act 2, the stabilized expedition returns to the same world. Human records classify it as unclaimed, underused or abandoned, but the protagonist recognizes the people living there.

This makes the mission immediately personal:

* Act 1 presents the world as a refuge;
* Act 2 presents it as a human resource opportunity;
* the protagonist knows that the resource site is someone else's home;
* the friend has to decide whether controlled extraction is acceptable when its consequences are visible.

The human expeditioner who eventually causes the release should be someone the protagonist trusts. Their family on the Human Homeworld is facing an immediate crisis, such as failing medical equipment, a collapsing hospital or severe resource shortages. The expedition's quota may directly affect whether their family receives help.

The expeditioner asks to access a sealed high-yield deposit and is refused because it lies outside the stabilized route or near dormant fungal growth. They take the risk anyway, believing that a single shortcut can save their family.

The deposit's energy and biological conditions allow the engineered fungus to grow explosively. The release overwhelms the stabilization system, spreads through the world and begins destabilizing the wider portal network.

The refugees the protagonist met in Act 1 are caught in the destruction. The expeditioner who caused the release becomes corrupted, making the disaster both a political failure and a personal tragedy.

### Act 2 endpoint

The portal network collapses while the expedition is trying to evacuate.

The protagonist and friend are caught in a portal together and land on a Concord world. The rest of the expedition team is left behind on the hostile resource world.

The friend immediately argues that they must reopen the portal to save their friends. The protagonist must decide whether to act immediately, refuse the connection or delay until they can create a stable route.

## Act 3 — The Door That Must Be Chosen

### The Concord refuge

The Concord world has achieved a successful fungal symbiosis. Its inhabitants are healthy and their society functions through a peaceful relationship with the local fungal network.

However, they cannot create stable portals. They either lost the required technology during the war or deliberately rejected it. The protagonists therefore need to combine Concord biological knowledge with human stabilization technology.

The first half of Act 3 is about gathering the materials and knowledge needed to stabilize a portal from the Concord world.

### The immediate rescue decision

The friend's position is emotionally understandable. The expeditioners are trapped, and he has already lost the protagonist once. He does not want another separation to become permanent.

The protagonist's response creates different immediate conditions without creating a completely separate campaign.

#### Severance response

The protagonist convinces the friend not to reopen the damaged portal because the connection could infect the Concord world.

The hostile resource world is not revisited. The expeditioners are presumed lost, and the protagonists travel directly to another known expedition world once they can create a stable route.

The Concord world avoids the immediate infection, but the protagonists must accept that they abandoned people who might have been saved.

#### Continuity response

The protagonist agrees to an emergency opening.

The portal reveals that the landing area on the hostile world has already been destroyed. Before the opening collapses, a mushroom-infected creature crosses into the Concord world and escapes.

This emergency connection is not stable enough for a rescue. The protagonists still need to complete the portal-building missions, but their work becomes increasingly difficult as the infected creature spreads through local wildlife and fungal growth.

The hostile resource world can be revisited later. By then, only one surviving expeditioner may remain, and that person is corrupted by the mushroom.

#### Concord response

The protagonist refuses to open the portal immediately but promises to return through a stable connection.

The protagonists gather materials and work with the Concord society before opening the route. They later return to the hostile resource world and find two stranded expeditioners:

* the expeditioner who caused the disaster, now corrupted;
* another expeditioner who is still alive and can be rescued.

This demonstrates that delay can be an active form of care rather than simple abandonment.

### The first stable Act 3 portal

Once the protagonists can stabilize their own portal, they can choose whether to return to the hostile resource world or travel directly to another world previously visited by the expedition that contains a human presence.

The hostile-world route provides additional rescue, evidence and consequences depending on the earlier decision. The direct route preserves the Severance decision and moves immediately toward the wider crisis.

Regardless of route, the protagonists eventually arrive at a known expedition world with human settlements, equipment and portal infrastructure.

There they discover that unstable portals have appeared across multiple worlds. The outbreak is no longer isolated to the resource world.

### The War of Open Doors

The portal outbreak creates a new war between Humans and Giants.

The conflict should not represent every Human fighting every Giant. It should contain several overlapping conflicts:

* human expeditioners defend supply routes and resource stations;
* human leaders try to preserve access to the worlds;
* Giant groups defend their own worlds and the small civilizations living among them;
* some Giants attack human facilities because they see another invasion beginning;
* some humans protect settlements rather than extract resources;
* corrupted Giants and infected creatures attack everyone;
* small civilizations are trapped between forces they did not choose.

The Giants have a small number of worlds containing their own settlements and dependent civilizations. The protagonists should see Giants protecting these communities, not simply defending territory for its own sake.

The war also gives the saved Giant group from Act 2 a narrative payoff. If the protagonists earned its trust, the group may help them move through Giant territory, negotiate with other communities or defend a portal stabilization site. Other Giant groups may remain hostile or refuse aid.

### The Concord world as home base

The Concord world becomes the protagonists' base for the second half of Act 3.

It provides a stable place where they can:

* return between missions;
* consult Concord fungal experts;
* study biological samples;
* build and repair stabilization equipment;
* meet human, Giant and other species representatives;
* decide which portal crisis to address next.

The friend handles the technical side of stabilization and research. The protagonist handles field missions, local relationships, rescues and dangerous anchor sites.

The resulting gameplay loop is:

> **Return to the Concord base → choose a portal crisis → stabilize an anchor or rescue a community → gather materials, samples and knowledge → return with new options.**

### Three forms of mission progress

Each Act 3 mission should provide one or more of three progress types.

#### Portal materials

The protagonists recover conductors, anchors, regulators and other components needed to stabilize the known portal network.

#### Biological samples

The protagonists study fungal strains, infected creatures, compatible hosts and symbiotic networks. Samples should be obtained through observation, voluntary contribution or carefully controlled recovery whenever possible.

Corrupted organisms may offer valuable information, but studying them should carry ethical and biological risks. A sample taken by force may provide data while damaging the relationship with that species.

#### Collaboration

Collaboration is more than a reputation score. It represents whether different communities are willing to share knowledge, provide consent, participate in experiments and help maintain the network.

Collaboration can be improved by:

* helping a community before asking for its resources;
* allowing local experts to set conditions;
* saving Giants rather than treating them as obstacles;
* sharing human technical knowledge;
* rescuing people even when it delays a quota;
* refusing to use sentient organisms as disposable tools;
* returning to communities and honoring earlier promises.

The strongest symbiosis research requires both biological information and collaboration. A stolen sample may reveal what a fungus can do, but cooperation reveals how it can be used without coercion.

### The Concord research program

As the protagonists complete missions, the friend and the Concord researchers develop a compatibility model for different species.

Humans and Giants are the primary species for now, but other intelligent species can contribute additional knowledge. Each species may have different:

* resistance to fungal infection;
* ability to communicate with the network;
* physical requirements for symbiosis;
* neurological or social risks;
* forms of consent and collective decision-making;
* ability to survive disconnection.

The goal is not to create a magical universal cure. More research and collaboration increase the chance that a species can survive a compatible symbiosis, but biology still matters.

The research can be represented through deterministic thresholds rather than random rolls:

* **Low compatibility:** symbiosis is lethal or viable only for rare, specially prepared hosts.
* **Moderate compatibility:** selected hosts can survive, but serious risks and transformations remain.
* **High compatibility:** a larger portion of the species can participate, provided that the process is voluntary and carefully monitored.

This makes the player's actions legible. The player should not complete every mission responsibly and then lose the result to an invisible random chance.

### Giants and other species as research partners

The Giant missions are a central part of the research system.

Helping an uncorrupted Giant group may provide:

* non-invasive biological information;
* knowledge of how Giant bodies resist fungal damage;
* insight into Giant relationships with local fungal networks;
* geological knowledge needed to stabilize anchors;
* a living partner for future symbiosis research.

Fighting corrupted Giants may provide different information about fungal transformation, but it should not be the only way to progress. The story should make clear that corrupted Giants are not representative of all Giants.

Other intelligent species can occupy the same system without becoming equally large political factions. One may possess a naturally cooperative fungal relationship. Another may be highly resistant but unable to communicate with the fungus. A third may know how to organize collective consent. Each can provide a piece of the larger solution.

### Stabilizing the known network

The protagonists should not need to physically visit every portal in existence. The outbreak can be represented as a resonance cascade flowing through a limited number of major anchor nodes.

The second half of Act 3 can therefore use a series of major missions:

1. **Evacuation:** rescue Humans, Giants and native civilians from a world being destabilized.
2. **Diplomacy:** convince a Giant group, human settlement or other species to cooperate.
3. **Anchor repair:** stabilize a portal site while dealing with corrupted creatures and environmental hazards.
4. **Containment:** prevent an infected route from spreading into another world.
5. **Network synchronization:** bring the major anchors into a stable relationship with the Concord biological buffer.

Stabilizing the known anchors stops the uncontrolled cascade and brings the remaining active portals under control. It does not automatically decide who should control the network afterward.

### The final use of the technology

The protagonists eventually find a way to stabilize the portal outbreak. The solution combines Human engineering, Giant structural knowledge, Concord symbiosis and information from other species.

This solves the immediate crisis but creates the final decision.

#### Continuity

The protagonists give the stabilization technology to the Humans.

The technology saves the Human Homeworld and keeps the portal network stable for the immediate future. Hospitals receive power, supply routes reopen and the human civilization avoids collapse.

However, the protagonists understand what will follow. Stable portals do not prevent:

* resource extraction;
* military occupation;
* political pressure;
* damage to other worlds;
* exploitation of small civilizations;
* the gradual weakening of safety restrictions.

The Humans will continue extracting from other worlds because their survival depends on it. The technology works, but it only buys time before the cycle begins again.

This is a tragic Continuity ending:

> **The protagonists save humanity today while knowingly enabling future harm.**

#### Severance

The protagonists use the stabilization knowledge to close or destroy the remaining portal anchors instead of giving the technology to the Humans.

The war ends because the routes disappear. Worlds become isolated again, and further exploitation is prevented. But human settlements lose access to resources, medicine and rescue routes, while stranded populations may be left behind.

Severance prevents another interworld catastrophe by accepting the suffering caused by permanent separation.

#### Concord

The protagonists combine stabilization technology with the Concord fungal network and distribute control between the connected worlds.

The resulting system can support voluntary, locally governed connections. It may allow more species to survive symbiosis, but it is not a perfect cure. Some hosts may die, some communities may reject it and some species may be biologically incompatible.

The quality of this ending depends on collaboration:

* low collaboration produces a fragile network that supports only a few species;
* moderate collaboration produces limited coexistence with serious risks;
* high collaboration allows Humans, Giants and other species to participate through consent-based agreements.

Concord is not the absence of danger. It is an attempt to make connection something that cannot exist without cooperation.

### Production-friendly branching

The major story locations can remain shared:

1. the Human Homeworld;
2. the Act 2 expedition worlds;
3. the hostile resource world;
4. the Concord home base;
5. the human-presence world where the war is discovered;
6. the major portal anchors;
7. the final network nexus.

The branches can change:

* whether the hostile world is revisited;
* whether an infected creature reaches the Concord world;
* whether zero, one or two expeditioners survive;
* which Giant groups trust the protagonists;
* which species contribute research;
* which routes are infected or safe;
* how difficult the portal missions become;
* what the friend believes by the final decision;
* how many species can participate in the Concord outcome.

The most useful persistent states are:

* **Portal stability:** how much of the known network is under control;
* **Infection state:** no infection, local infection or spreading infection;
* **Expedition survivors:** the number and condition of the stranded team;
* **Giant collaboration:** hostile, cautious, allied or actively assisting;
* **Cross-species collaboration:** the quality of the shared research and consent process;
* **Friend's conviction:** Continuity, doubt or Concord-oriented cooperation.

The protagonist and friend should still reach the same broad final crisis in every playthrough. The player's choices determine who is present, who trusts them, what the protagonists know and what each final option costs.

The central design principle is:

> **Machines can stabilize portals. Only collaboration can stabilize relationships.**

# VII. Gameplay World and Enemy Ecology

This section translates the world history and the three philosophies into a gameplay-facing roster of environments, native species, enemy roles and combat abilities.

The roster is a starting point rather than a final canon list. The first seven worlds extend the existing world stories. The final three are additional world types that broaden the traversal and combat vocabulary while remaining compatible with the portal, fungal and interworld history.

The central gameplay rule is:

> **Every enemy should create a movement question, not merely deal damage.**

The player should be deciding whether to grapple upward, cross a line of fire, wall-run around danger, release momentum early, attack from above, preserve a recovery route or deliberately enter a dangerous space to reach a weak point.

## 1. Universal Grapple Assumption

Almost all surfaces and targets are grapple-able by default.

The player should not normally search for a small set of approved anchor points. Buildings, floors, walls, branches, fungal growths, ice, machinery, debris, platforms, enemy bodies and moving targets can all be grapple-able when their target contract permits it.

The important distinction is:

> **A surface can remain grapple-able while being dangerous, obstructed, slow, moving or contested.**

An enemy or world feature should usually change the value or behavior of a grapple target rather than remove access to it entirely.

### Default grapple behavior

* Ordinary world geometry is grapple-able unless explicitly marked otherwise.
* Enemy-created roots, crystals, walls, webs, pillars and barricades are grapple-able by default.
* Moving enemies can be grapple-able when their `Grappleable` contract supports a moving hit point and target invalidation.
* A grapple-able target may be heavy, elastic, unstable, adhesive, hazardous or resistant without becoming invalid.
* Glowing knots, relay nodes and other visual markers should communicate especially useful routes, not basic eligibility.
* Floors and other broad surfaces may remain grapple-able, but hazards should make the player consider whether pulling toward them is worthwhile.

### Explicit exceptions

Truly invalid surfaces should be rare, intentional and readable. Possible exceptions include:

* a portal surface that has collapsed;
* a temporary Severance device that suppresses resonance;
* a world substance that actively rejects the harness;
* a destructible surface that cannot support the player;
* a story-specific transition where the target is disappearing.

An invalid target should visibly change state, produce a clear audio cue and update the grapple cursor. It should not silently fail.

### Anchor ecology

Each level should contain an ecology of grapple targets rather than a collection of isolated anchor points:

* **Permanent structural targets:** walls, trunks, cliffs, towers and large ruins that remain available throughout the encounter.
* **Modified targets:** surfaces that enemies make sticky, burning, electrified, unstable or difficult to approach.
* **Temporary targets:** obstacles or growths created during combat that can also become useful routes.
* **Moving targets:** enemies, creatures, platforms or vehicles that provide advanced, optional routes.
* **Recovery targets:** nearby surfaces that allow the player to recover from a missed jump, knockback or poor release.

Every major combat space should preserve a low route, a high route and a lateral route. An enemy may make one route dangerous or inefficient, but the fight should not normally remove the entire movement vocabulary.

## 2. Combat Role Framework

The existing game design defines melee mobs, ranged mobs, elite enemies and bosses. The following sub-roles make those categories more useful during encounter design.

| Role | Movement question | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|
| Melee pursuer | Can I keep changing position instead of fighting passively on the ground? | Pounces, charges, sweeps, grabs and short-range cones |
| Bruiser | Can I preserve momentum while avoiding a high-impact attack? | Armor, knockback, large melee attacks and destructible cover |
| Direct ranged | Where can I cross the enemy's line of sight safely? | Bolts, beams, spears and rail shots |
| Artillery / lobber | Where will I be when the delayed attack lands? | Arcing shots, ground circles and falling debris |
| Space controller | Which route is still available? | Sticky surfaces, gas, temporary walls and surface states |
| Disruptor | Can I recover when my velocity or position changes? | Tethers, pulls, knockback and anchor modification |
| Anti-wall / aerial | Is my wall or high position actually safe? | Wall climbing, dive attacks, air mines and vertical beams |
| Support / network | Which enemy must I interrupt before attacking the damage dealer? | Healing links, shields, buffs and target marks |
| Scout / marker | Can I break the enemy's planned attack before it begins? | Tracking, alarms and predicted-position marks |
| Elite hybrid | Can I answer two kinds of pressure without losing flow? | Combined melee, ranged, control and defensive tools |
| Boss | Can I learn the arena's pattern and create a high-value opening? | Phase changes, route changes, weak points and large AOE |

Intelligent species should not be enemies by default. An uncorrupted species can be neutral or friendly, while a factional combatant, territorial guardian, corrupted individual or defensive construct can use the same species silhouette as an enemy. This distinction is important to the setting's argument that hostile behavior is not proof that an entire species is evil.

## 3. Reusable Enemy Ability Mechanics

The following mechanics can be reused across worlds with different visual, biological and cultural explanations.

### 3.1 Adhesive surfaces

Resin, webs, frost, sap, fungal mucus or mineral sludge can reduce acceleration and air control, prevent wall-running or make landings slide.

The surface remains grapple-able by default. Its danger comes from how it changes the player's movement after contact. The player can grapple to it as an emergency choice, escape to a clean surface or attack the creature producing it.

### 3.2 Temporary obstacle growth

Roots, crystal ridges, ice walls, obsidian columns and fungal barricades rise from visibly marked locations.

The obstacle should usually be destructible, avoidable, climbable or grapple-able once it forms. Its purpose is to block a line of sight, split one route into two, force a different attack angle or create a new vertical surface.

### 3.3 Harpoon or tether

A projectile or melee attack attaches a line to the player. It can slow horizontal movement, pull the player toward the enemy, disturb a release or force the player to move around an obstacle to break line of sight.

The player should be able to escape by grappling behind cover, attacking the tether head, breaking the line or using a wall jump. A tether should not automatically cancel an active grapple without warning.

### 3.4 Knockback and displacement

Large melee attacks, shockwaves, charges and explosions alter the player's velocity.

Displacement should create a new movement problem rather than a stun-lock sequence. Nearby recovery surfaces and preserved player control make the resulting scramble part of the game's skill expression.

### 3.5 Predictive mark

An enemy marks the player's current position, intended landing position or likely grapple release point. After a delay, the marked location receives an attack.

This punishes predictable movement without punishing movement itself. Changing direction, releasing early, crossing to another surface or using a different altitude should defeat the prediction.

### 3.6 Direct lane shot

A bolt, beam, spear or rail shot travels in a straight line. The attack requires line of sight and has a visible aiming line, charging sound or emissive weapon state.

The player can cross the lane, use cover, grapple around the enemy or attack during the firing recovery.

### 3.7 Arcing bombardment

An enemy fires over cover or toward a future position. The projectile creates a ground or surface AOE when it lands.

Arcing attacks prevent the player from treating the nearest wall as a complete solution. The player must continue moving, change altitude or leave the predicted landing area.

### 3.8 Rotating plane or line sweep

A beam, wind blade, fungal wave or electrical plane sweeps through part of the arena.

The player can move with the sweep, cross behind it, grapple around it or use a wall-stick pause to wait for an opening. This is especially effective for bosses because the danger remains spatially understandable in a large arena.

### 3.9 Visibility obstruction

Smoke, spores, snow, dust, ash or mist reduces long-range visibility.

This should not become a full-screen blind effect. Silhouettes, attack flashes, sound direction, nearby geometry and grapple feedback should remain readable. The purpose is to make route reading harder, not to hide the answer from the player.

### 3.10 Damage gas or drifting hazard

A gas cloud, spore field, volcanic vapor or corrosive mist deals damage over time and drifts according to wind or enemy movement.

The cloud should have a readable boundary and a ramp-up period. The player can cross briefly to preserve momentum, grapple over it or eliminate the source before proceeding.

### 3.11 Airburst and aerial mine lattice

Floating spores, lightning seeds, crystal mines, expanding airburst rings and rotating projectile clusters place danger above the floor.

This prevents grappling upward from becoming a universal answer. The player must choose another altitude, move sideways or use a wall as cover.

### 3.12 Anti-wall reach

Some enemies can challenge a wall-sticking player by climbing, leaping to the wall, throwing a short-range harpoon, firing a vertical attack or changing the wall's movement response.

Anti-wall attacks should be highly readable and committed. If every enemy can instantly reach any wall, wall interaction stops being useful.

### 3.13 Anchor modification

An enemy can change the response of one or more grapple targets without necessarily making them invalid. A surface may pull toward a dangerous point, create sideways drag, conduct electricity or become unstable after prolonged contact.

Full anchor blackout should be rare and reserved for explicit technology or ecology, such as a Severance device or a portal guardian. It should affect a small local area, be telegraphed and leave other routes available.

### 3.14 Support tether

A support enemy links itself to another enemy, providing healing, armor, faster recovery, stagger resistance or shared damage reduction.

The player can break the link by moving behind cover, attacking the support enemy, destroying an intermediate relay or forcing the linked enemy away.

### 3.15 Decoy or echo

The enemy creates false bodies, false grapple targets, delayed afterimages or copies of the player's previous position.

The deception should have a consistent tell: a different color, sound, shadow, outline or movement response. The mechanic should reward observation rather than make targeting arbitrary.

### 3.16 Wind, suction and directional vectors

An enemy changes the direction of the player's velocity instead of simply slowing it. A storm creature may create crosswinds, a giant may inhale toward a cavern and a fungal bloom may push the player away from its core.

The player can counter with a lateral grapple, wall-run, angled jump or early release that preserves the desired vector.

### 3.17 Surface state changes

A surface can temporarily become burning, freezing, electrified, brittle, slippery or resonant.

The player should be able to recognize the state and choose whether to cross quickly, grapple over it, use it briefly for momentum or wait for it to expire. Surface states should change the value of a route without permanently removing the route.

## 4. Corruption and Intelligent Factions

Corruption should modify a familiar animal or person rather than turning every infected creature into the same generic monster.

A shared corruption system can use several modifiers:

* **Overgrowth:** roots, additional limbs, barriers or fungal armor.
* **Spore:** visibility clouds, poison, delayed bursts or drifting hazards.
* **Resonance:** pulses, repeated attacks and network links.
* **Neural distortion:** decoys, false targets and unstable movement.
* **Fungal regeneration:** healing tethers, recoverable armor or re-growing body parts.
* **Portal instability:** short phase shifts between marked surfaces or anchor points.

An uncorrupted animal can remain visible in the environment as ordinary wildlife. A corrupted version should retain its original silhouette and behavior while gaining one or two fungal modifiers.

Examples:

* An uncorrupted Hushhorn is a harmless grazer; a corrupted Hushhorn becomes a root-charging melee enemy.
* An uncorrupted Giant is a cautious NPC; a corrupted Giant becomes an anti-wall elite.
* An uncorrupted Naru maintains a peaceful network; a defensive Naru construct becomes a support or controller enemy.

Intelligent species can occupy several narrative states:

* **Neutral:** willing to trade information or allow passage but not yet committed to the player.
* **Friendly:** provides routes, samples, equipment, dialogue or combat assistance.
* **Factional enemy:** fights because of ideology, territorial defense, fear or political allegiance rather than biological evil.
* **Corrupted:** retains fragments of identity while fungal changes alter its abilities and judgment.
* **Defensive construct:** a non-personal guardian created by a community, research site or portal system.

This allows the player to fight a species in one encounter, speak with another member of the same species later and understand why the difference exists.

### World 1 — The Last Garden

**Setting and philosophy:**

The glowing fungal jungle and ruined agricultural settlement described in the existing World 1 story. It is a Severance world where burning and removing danger has protected a small settlement, but may also destroy the only organisms capable of adapting to the new ecology.

The Keeper, her partially infected son and the Hunter remain important human characters. The son should not automatically become a boss; his uncertain condition is more effective as a moral relationship than as a required combat encounter.

**Terrain and grapple ecology:**

* Giant fungal trunks and ridges.
* Hanging roots and canopy branches.
* Ruined greenhouse towers and irrigation structures.
* Burn-cleared ground surrounded by dense growth.
* Temporary fungal walls and hardened growths.

All of these are grapple-able by default. Living surfaces may pull unevenly, release spores on contact or become sticky after a Weaver modifies them. The player should move between low clearings, mid-height roots and upper canopy routes.

**Native species and NPC potential:**

* **Lumenari:** intelligent fungal-symbiotic garden caretakers. They can be neutral or friendly, defensive of their gardens, or corrupted.
* **Hushhorns:** deer-like grazers with glowing fungal antlers. Corrupted individuals become chargers or spore carriers.
* **Thornmantises:** large climbing predators.
* **Spore Kites:** flying scavengers that drift above the canopy.
* **Rootbacks:** slow armored herbivores that create paths through dense growth.

**Enemy roster:**

* **Rootstalker — melee skirmisher / anti-wall.** A corrupted Thornmantis circles around trunks, produces a bright leaf-flare telegraph and pounces. A successful hit briefly creates a root ring around the player. It can climb toward a wall-sticking player, but a missed pounce leaves it exposed. The player should re-grapple to the side or overhead and punish the recovery.
* **Spore Kite — aerial ranged enemy.** It fires a three-shot arcing spore volley and drops a pollen cloud at the predicted landing area. The player can move behind a trunk, change altitude or grapple to a side branch. The Kite is vulnerable immediately after the volley.
* **Mycelial Weaver — controller / support.** It sprays sticky resin over one landing surface, grows a temporary wall across a common route and tethers nearby corrupted creatures to restore health. Interrupting the growth exposes it. The finished wall remains grapple-able and may create an alternate route.
* **Bloombound Hunter — elite melee/ranged hybrid.** A corrupted Lumenari or human hunter uses a thorn spear at close range and tracking spores at longer range. The mark detonates at the player's likely release point. The elite is strongest when the player repeats the same grapple arc and weakest after two prediction failures.

**Boss — The Garden Heart:**

The boss is the runaway central bloom rather than the partially infected son.

* Phase one uses root sweeps and circular ground growth.
* Phase two adds spore rain and elevated sacs that must be reached through the canopy.
* Phase three creates a limited number of safe approach routes while the lower arena fills with expanding fungal growth.

The core becomes vulnerable after the player causes the bloom to overextend a root limb. The encounter teaches that dangerous growth can also be used as grapple-able terrain.

### World 2 — The Hero of the Wall

**Setting and philosophy:**

A fortified human settlement built into a gigantic basalt canyon. Its legendary commander has repeatedly defended the population from fungal-mutated creatures, embodying the strongest emotional argument for Continuity.

The settlement should have combat spaces on the exterior wall, inside defensive corridors and above the artillery platforms. The Talari can be part of the local population or an indigenous group whose cooperation has been strained by the fortress's expansion.

**Terrain and grapple ecology:**

* Crenellations and buttresses.
* Siege towers and wall windows.
* Hanging maintenance cables and banners.
* Vertical basalt faces.
* Broken bridges and artillery platforms.

The wall itself is a continuous grapple surface. The tactical challenge is not finding a legal anchor but deciding whether the next grapple crosses a firing lane, lands on a weak ledge or places the player within reach of an anti-wall defender.

**Native species and NPC potential:**

* **Talari:** intelligent cliff-dwellers with gliding membranes. They can be neutral or friendly scouts, messengers and guides, or factional enemies if the fortress has mistreated them.
* **Wallhorns:** heavy cliff herbivores.
* **Bellwings:** small aerial predators that coordinate through sound.
* **Stone mites:** swarming insects that live in basalt.
* **Canyon eels:** long gliding predators that travel through thermal currents.

**Enemy roster:**

* **Wall Lancer — melee bruiser.** A shielded soldier or corrupted Talari charges along a ledge, then performs a sweeping hook designed to knock the player away from the wall. The player can grapple across the ledge or jump the sweep. The Lancer is vulnerable after striking a wall or barricade.
* **Bolt Emplacer — direct ranged enemy.** It occupies a crenellation and charges a straight-line bolt. A visible aiming line gives the player time to cross the lane, move behind a buttress or attack from a higher wall face.
* **Spore Mortar — artillery / controller.** It fires over the wall and creates a sticky resin patch or short-lived smoke cloud. The attack makes passive ground routes unsafe without making the upper wall inaccessible.
* **Bellwing Spotter — aerial scout / support.** It circles above the player and marks them with a distinctive chime. Marked players are targeted by delayed bolts from wall emplacements. The Spotter can be killed, hidden from or interrupted before the mark completes.
* **Infected Wallguard — elite anti-wall enemy.** A human or Talari defender with fungal growth in its armor uses a shield bash, short harpoon and anti-wall leap. Its fungal node is exposed when the shield bash hits a solid surface.

**Boss — The Last Commander:**

This is the infected commander from the existing world concept.

* Phase one is a shield-and-spear duel on a broad wall face.
* Phase two activates mortar batteries and introduces a harpoon that can reach wall-sticking players.
* Phase three is an overdrive chase where the commander breaks sections of cover but exposes coolant and fungal weak points.

The commander should still move like a skilled protector. The tragedy comes from seeing a heroic defensive pattern continue after the infection has begun to replace judgment with survival instinct.

### World 3 — The Severed World

**Setting and philosophy:**

A salt basin and canyon region damaged by the closing of its portal. The world expresses the cost of Severance: destroying the connection prevented a fungal catastrophe but also cut off medicine, ecological technology and family members who could not escape.

The Archivist and Farmer should represent opposing memories of the same decision. Neither needs to be lying, and neither should be reduced to a simple faction quest.

**Terrain and grapple ecology:**

* Tall salt arches and mineral spires.
* Exposed portal pylons.
* Broken aqueducts and archive platforms.
* Canyon walls and sinkhole rims.
* Suspended pieces of collapsed infrastructure.

The player can grapple almost everything, but many surfaces are fragile, resonant or exposed to line-of-sight attacks. The key movement decision is whether to cross open ground quickly or use a longer route through vertical cover.

**Native species and NPC potential:**

* **Serrak:** intelligent burrowing people who preserve memories in mineral structures. They can be neutral or friendly archivists, or hostile Null Scribes who believe all resonance technology must be suppressed.
* **Glassback skitters:** fast crystalline insects.
* **Dune rays:** broad-winged creatures that surf thermal currents.
* **Salt eels:** burrowing ambush predators.
* **Vault tortoises:** slow herbivores with mineral shells.

**Enemy roster:**

* **Burrowjaw — melee ambusher.** It disappears beneath the ground, producing a moving dust ridge before emerging under the player's predicted landing point. Grappling to a canyon wall is often safer than remaining on the marked ground.
* **Prism Stalker — direct ranged enemy.** It fires a narrow beam that can refract once through a salt crystal. The player must read both the enemy and the crystal geometry. Breaking or moving around the crystal changes the firing lane.
* **Dune Lobber — artillery / controller.** It fires arcing globs that create temporary sink zones. These zones reduce acceleration and slowly pull the player toward their center. The Lobber is vulnerable while reloading.
* **Null Scribe — intelligent controller.** It marks one or two portal anchors with dead resonance, temporarily changing them into invalid grapple targets. The affected surfaces darken and pulse before the effect begins. Other surfaces remain available, and the Scribe is vulnerable while maintaining the mark.
* **Saltback Colossus — elite bruiser.** It rolls through the arena, breaks salt cover and creates a crystal ridge when it stops. Its belly is exposed after it misses a charge, rewarding the player for baiting its path.

**Boss — The Empty Archive:**

An ancient portal guardian fused with salt, mineral deposits and unstable fungal material.

* It alternates between underground movement and exposed archive chambers.
* Its surface phase creates fissures and refracted beam lanes.
* Its second phase adds suction toward a sinkhole and short anchor blackouts.
* The core becomes vulnerable when the player baits a charge into the correct ancient relay.

The boss represents a decision that was simultaneously salvation and catastrophe depending on whose family is remembered.

### World 4 — The Giant's Memory

**Setting and philosophy:**

A highland world of waterfalls, Giant ruins and monsoon cliffs. It is the clearest encounter with the Golden Age of Human-Giant exploration and the collapse of the original ideal.

Scale should be a mechanical feature. Human-sized ledges, Giant stairs, broken bridges and enormous relics should all be viable routes, but each should create different sightlines and attack angles.

**Terrain and grapple ecology:**

* Giant staircases and bridge ribs.
* Waterfall chains and cliffside platforms.
* Stone rings and hanging ruins.
* Large moving creatures crossing the valleys.
* Relic structures designed for the original Pathfinder harness.

The world should provide many permanent grapple surfaces. Moving wildlife can be optional advanced targets rather than a requirement for normal traversal.

**Native species and NPC potential:**

* **Giants:** intelligent and culturally diverse. They can be cautious, neutral, friendly, hostile or corrupted. Most Giants should be NPCs rather than enemies.
* **Thunderhorns:** large mountain herbivores.
* **Cliff mantas:** gliding creatures that move between waterfalls.
* **Rain serpents:** long aerial predators.
* **Stoneback crawlers:** armored animals that nest in ruined architecture.

**Enemy roster:**

* **Thunderhorn — melee bruiser.** It telegraphs a charge with a deep call and ground vibration. The charge destroys light cover and ends in a vulnerable recovery if it hits a wall or bridge support.
* **Rain Manta — aerial harrier.** It dives through the player's route and releases a fan of water blades. It can be grappled as an advanced moving target, but doing so may expose the player to a second aerial creature.
* **Boulder Slinger — ranged artillery.** A corrupted Giant or hostile Giant combatant throws arcing stones. Impacts create temporary obstructions or shockwaves rather than simply dealing damage.
* **Anchor Caller — support / controller.** It uses a resonant horn to strengthen corrupted creatures and create directional wind along a bridge. Interrupting the horn cancels both effects.
* **Mossbound Giant — elite anti-wall enemy.** This infected Giant climbs vertical surfaces, performs a long arm sweep and releases spores from its shoulder. Its weak point is exposed after the player causes it to strike an ancient relay.

**Boss — The Cloudbreaker:**

A corrupted Giant protector who still follows fragments of an old defensive command.

* Phase one uses stomps, arm sweeps and thrown stone.
* Phase two covers parts of the mountain in rain curtains and forces the player onto upper rings.
* Phase three adds a resonant roar that collapses the lower route.

The Giant becomes vulnerable after repeatedly attacking a relay used by the player as a grapple target. This makes the fight about redirecting a protector's strength rather than simply overpowering it.

### World 5 — The Joined

**Setting and philosophy:**

A symbiotic wetland and canopy society that represents the strongest argument for Concord. The world appears safe and beautiful, but social life depends on a fungal network that makes disconnection physically and socially costly.

The Dissenter, the Caretaker and the Dissenter's Partner should remain central. Combat in this world can involve defensive constructs, factional disputes and network-driven creatures rather than treating the entire society as corrupted.

**Terrain and grapple ecology:**

* Braided root bridges.
* Fungal columns and floating garden islands.
* Elastic living pads and suspended pods.
* Network relay nodes and shallow water channels.
* Living walls and roots that change response when the network is disturbed.

The living environment is almost universally grapple-able. Some surfaces can pull gently toward the network, become adhesive or create a pulse when the player releases from them.

**Native species and NPC potential:**

* **Naru:** intelligent amphibious or semi-aquatic people who participate in a fungal social network. They can be friendly, neutral, dissenting or defensive.
* **Chorus frogs:** small wildlife whose calls synchronize.
* **Ribbon grazers:** long-bodied wetland herbivores.
* **Lantern mantas:** slow flying filter-feeders.
* **Root crabs:** armored scavengers that maintain fungal structures.

**Enemy roster:**

* **Chorus Stalker — synchronized melee pack.** Several creatures attack on a repeating rhythm. Their attacks become less coordinated when separated or when one member is staggered. The behavior can use authored timing markers rather than advanced free-form group AI.
* **Pulse Weaver — ranged disruptor.** It fires resonance needles that add momentum drag rather than a hard stun. The player can keep moving, but direction changes become more difficult for a short time.
* **Tendril Custodian — controller.** It grows living walls and adhesive root pads. Finished roots remain grapple-able and can become alternate routes, allowing the enemy to reshape the level in both harmful and useful ways.
* **Linkbearer — support enemy.** It creates healing or shielding tethers between nearby enemies. The player can break the line by moving around a root pillar or attacking the Linkbearer.
* **Consensus Guardian — elite hybrid.** This defensive construct records the player's most-used route and places a pulse hazard at the last grapple endpoint. It encourages route variation without making any route permanently wrong.

**Boss — The Living Consensus:**

A network defense body created by the local fungal society.

* Phase one uses synchronized pulses and support links.
* Phase two changes the shape of root bridges and grows barriers across the most obvious route.
* Phase three creates a repeating wave pattern across the entire arena.

The player must reach and disconnect several relays before the central body becomes vulnerable. A non-lethal shutdown or dialogue resolution can remain possible, reinforcing that the encounter is about autonomy rather than exterminating Concord life.

### World 6 — The Gentle Mind

**Setting and philosophy:**

Underground caverns and an abandoned Concord research site built around a fungal organism with clear individual consciousness. This world explores Concord's temptation to treat understanding as ownership.

The Gentle Mind should not automatically be the villain. The Researcher, Guardian and Fungal Being can create conflicting objectives, while containment organisms and failed experiments provide the combat encounters.

**Terrain and grapple ecology:**

* Large vertical sinkholes.
* Stalactites, stalagmites and deep shaft walls.
* Laboratory gantries and magnetic rails.
* Luminous roots and suspended containment structures.
* Open research chambers connected by narrow side routes.

Rock, metal, roots and containment structures are grapple-able. Some targets can be unstable, move under the Gentle Mind's influence or create a dangerous resonance response when used repeatedly.

**Native species and NPC potential:**

* **The Gentle Mind:** a sentient fungal organism with individual consciousness. It can be neutral or friendly and should not automatically be treated as an enemy.
* **Cave striders:** large wall-climbing predators.
* **Echo bats:** aerial creatures that navigate through sound.
* **Gas leeches:** parasitic organisms that attach to hosts.
* **Crystal moths:** harmless wildlife that can become corrupted.
* Human researchers and Guardians can be allies, neutral NPCs or factional enemies.

**Enemy roster:**

* **Cave Strider — melee anti-wall enemy.** It climbs toward wall-sticking players and attacks with a two-stage pincer strike. If the second strike misses, it hangs briefly from the wall and can be attacked.
* **Echo Bat — aerial visibility disruptor.** It releases a sonic pulse that reveals the player's position to other enemies and then creates a small spore haze. The player can move behind rock, attack during the charge or change altitude.
* **Gas Leech — close-range control enemy.** It attaches to the player, adds movement drag and deals mild damage over time. The player can shake it off through a traversal action, attack it directly or grapple away before the drain completes.
* **Resonance Turret — direct ranged enemy.** An old research device projects a rotating beam across a chamber. The player can move around the beam, use pillars as cover or reach the turret from above.
* **Prototype Host — elite hybrid.** A failed experiment creates a decoy body at the player's previous position and then attacks with tendrils. The real body has a different sound and shadow, preserving fair identification.

**Boss — The Containment Crown:**

A protective fungal body grown by the Gentle Mind to prevent further experimentation.

* Its early attacks restrain rather than kill: root cages, slow fields and gas curtains.
* Its second phase adds airburst spores and false grapple responses.
* Its final phase drops the containment shell and exposes the central biological core.

The boss can be disabled rather than destroyed. This preserves the ethical theme while still providing a complete boss encounter.

### World 7 — The Lifeline

**Setting and philosophy:**

A magma world operated as a Continuity resource site. It contains a functioning human extraction facility, a native civilization around geothermal zones and the immediate pressures that make the human administrator's decisions understandable.

The resource operation should include workers who were born on the world and have never seen the Human Homeworld. The player should be able to understand both the native resistance and the human fear of missed quotas.

**Terrain and grapple ecology:**

* Basalt columns and obsidian shelves.
* Cooling towers, mine cranes and drilling rigs.
* Cable spans and hanging industrial platforms.
* Geothermal vents and lava channels.
* Portal machinery built into volcanic chambers.

Basalt, machinery, cables and cooling structures are grapple-able. Lava may remain grapple-able but should be a hazardous or unstable target rather than a universally invalid one. The player can use a dangerous surface briefly to preserve momentum.

**Native species and NPC potential:**

* **Cindrel:** intelligent heat-adapted people who live around volcanic vents. They can be neutral, friendly, resistant to extraction or corrupted.
* **Ember drakes:** aggressive flying predators.
* **Magma eels:** creatures that travel through lava channels.
* **Ash moths:** harmless in small numbers but dangerous in clouds.
* **Basalt tortoises:** slow armored herbivores that tolerate extreme heat.

**Enemy roster:**

* **Ember Drake — melee / aerial skirmisher.** It dives toward the player and leaves a burning trail on the surface. The player can grapple to a cool structure, cross the trail quickly or attack after the dive.
* **Magma Spitter — ranged artillery.** It fires molten projectiles that create temporary hazard pools. Direct shots are faster; arcing shots are slower but reshape the route.
* **Vent Burrower — controller / ambusher.** It erupts from marked vents and launches the player upward or sideways. Its eruptions create obsidian columns that remain grapple-able.
* **Drill Sentry — human industrial ranged enemy.** It fires a direct beam and deploys delayed marker mines around the player's likely landing point. The player can reach its control housing or use the mining structure as cover.
* **Breachworker — elite corrupted human.** The corrupted expeditioner wears a heavy drilling rig, uses a harpoon pull and performs a close-range spinning drill attack. Heat buildup eventually exposes coolant lines and creates an attack window.

**Boss — The Quota Engine:**

This can be the corrupted Breachworker amplified by the extraction rig or a giant fungal-industrial organism created during the release.

* Phase one uses drill charges and direct industrial weapons.
* Phase two activates lava vents and forces the player across cranes and cooling towers.
* Phase three overheats the rig, making coolant valves and the fungal core vulnerable from different grapple angles.

The boss should make the disaster personal rather than presenting extraction as an abstract evil machine.

### World 8 — The Crown of Branches

**Setting and philosophy:**

A true treetop world above an endless forest. Unlike The Last Garden, this is not a fungal ecological takeover. The world has always supported a native civilization whose homes, routes and social boundaries exist in the upper canopy.

It can be placed early in Act 1 as a world that teaches the player how much freedom the grapple system offers when the ecology itself is vertical.

**Terrain and grapple ecology:**

* Enormous branch collars and hollow trunks.
* Hanging seed pods and rope bridges.
* Canopy towers and wind gaps.
* Thick vines used as recovery surfaces.
* Open sky beneath the canopy.

Branches, pods, vines, trunks and platforms are all grapple-able. The challenge is selecting a stable route through a three-dimensional branch network while enemies contest the player’s most direct lines.

**Native species and NPC potential:**

* **Aeral:** intelligent gliding people who build settlements in the upper canopy. They can be neutral or friendly, while local hunters may become hostile if the player violates their boundaries.
* **Vine raptors:** large branch predators.
* **Needlewings:** aerial flock creatures.
* **Webspinners:** territorial arachnid-like wildlife.
* **Canopy hoppers:** small herbivores that leap between branches.
* **Sky rays:** slow flying filter-feeders.

**Enemy roster:**

* **Vine Raptor — melee pouncer.** It moves around a branch rather than approaching directly, then launches a diagonal pounce. A missed attack leaves it hanging from a vine.
* **Needlewing Flock — aerial ranged enemy.** The flock forms a line and fires a crossing volley. The player can move beneath a branch, pass between the shots or attack the flock's roost.
* **Webspinner — controller.** It creates sticky web curtains between branches and under platforms. Webs make the direct route slower while leaving a side route open.
* **Canopy Caller — scout / support.** It marks a branch or seed pod, then causes nearby creatures to fire seed bombs at that location. It can be interrupted before the mark completes.
* **Boughbreaker — elite bruiser.** A corrupted large herbivore tears through branch segments and creates falling debris. Destruction reveals thicker trunk surfaces that become new grapple routes.

**Boss — The Crown Predator:**

An apex glider or corrupted canopy matriarch.

* Phase one is a pursuit around the outer crown.
* Phase two adds falling seed bombs, wind gusts and web storms.
* Phase three causes the creature to dive through the canopy and exposes an underside weak point.

Grappling to the boss can be an advanced option, but the reliable strategy remains using the tree geometry to make it miss.

### World 9 — The Blue Silence

**Setting and philosophy:**

A glacier world of frozen cliffs, buried portal infrastructure and isolated communities. It can express the emotional cost of preserving a world by cutting it off from the wider network.

The glacier should not be a flat snowy landscape. The player should move through vertical ice faces, crevasses, hanging frozen waterfalls, geothermal pockets and research structures embedded in the ice.

**Terrain and grapple ecology:**

* Exposed rock and ice hooks.
* Frozen cables and research towers.
* Geothermal pylons and blue resonance crystals.
* Glacier walls and suspended platforms.
* Ice caves with multiple vertical shafts.

Ice and rock are grapple-able by default. Some ice surfaces increase sliding or reduce turning precision, while resonance crystals provide more stable pull behavior. Surfaces should not become universally invalid simply because they are frozen.

**Native species and NPC potential:**

* **Nivek:** intelligent cold-adapted people who travel between ice settlements. They can be neutral or friendly and may know which structures are stable.
* **Frost elk:** large herbivores.
* **Ice worms:** burrowing predators.
* **Mirror owls:** aerial hunters that blend into aurora light.
* **Floe seals:** aquatic or semi-aquatic wildlife around meltwater.
* **Rimebacks:** armored creatures with ice-plated shells.

**Enemy roster:**

* **Frostclaw — melee skirmisher.** It applies a short-lived frost mark that reduces air-control response rather than stopping movement. Repeated hits make the player brittle, so avoiding a second contact matters.
* **Rimeback — bruiser / controller.** It rolls across the arena and creates an ice ridge when it stops. The ridge blocks a direct route but remains grapple-able as a new surface.
* **Snowveil Owl — aerial visibility enemy.** It releases a snow cloud, then attacks from a different angle. Its silhouette and wing sound remain readable inside the cloud.
* **Cryospire Caster — ranged / controller.** It fires a direct crystal lance and freezes strips of surface in a line. Frozen surfaces increase sliding and reduce turning precision without completely removing movement.
* **Icebound Giant — elite anti-wall enemy.** A corrupted Giant climbs ice faces, shatters platforms and uses a long-range frost breath. Its chest node is exposed after it breaks a marked ice pillar.

**Boss — The Blue Warden:**

An ancient guardian near a buried portal anchor.

* Phase one uses charges, frost breath and ground freeze.
* Phase two introduces an aurora beam sweeping through a vertical plane.
* Phase three shatters portions of the arena and forces the player between geothermal anchors.

The weak point opens when the Warden is lured near a thermal relay. The player is rewarded for preserving warm routes rather than attacking whenever possible.

### World 10 — The Thunder Shelf

**Setting and philosophy:**

A world of floating basalt islands, storm systems, old electromagnetic infrastructure and native glider settlements. This is an additional world focused on aerial movement, directional forces and the ancient Human technology that made the portal network possible.

The Kesh can explain how a society adapted to a dangerous environment without needing to control every part of it. Human or fungal attempts to regulate the storms can provide the world’s historical conflict.

**Terrain and grapple ecology:**

* Lightning pylons and suspended mass-driver rings.
* Hanging cables and basalt arches.
* Floating platforms and storm towers.
* Wrecked electromagnetic machinery.
* Large moving cloud fauna.

Pylons, cables, rings, machinery and floating rock are grapple-able. Wind and electricity modify the movement response. Some moving creatures are optional high-skill targets for experienced players.

**Native species and NPC potential:**

* **Kesh:** intelligent glider people who maintain storm routes and weather structures. They can be neutral or friendly, while militant groups or corrupted individuals may become enemies.
* **Cloud rays:** large floating animals.
* **Coil serpents:** flexible aerial predators.
* **Bolt beetles:** small creatures that accumulate electrical charge.
* **Storm gulls:** aggressive flocking birds.
* **Rain spiders:** creatures that build suspended web nests between islands.

**Enemy roster:**

* **Coil Serpent — melee disruptor.** It wraps around a cable, pylon or occasionally the player's tether and constricts. The player can change anchors, attack the body or use an obstruction to break the line.
* **Bolt Manta — aerial direct ranged enemy.** It charges a lightning line and fires through the player's current flight path. After firing, it performs a dive that can be avoided or used as an optional moving grapple target.
* **Thunder Nest — controller.** It activates electrical links between several pylons. The danger forms a visible triangle or corridor rather than an invisible damage field. Disabling one nest opens a different route.
* **Wind Herder — support / controller.** It creates gust lanes that push both the player and nearby enemies. It can shield allies while the wind is active, making it a priority target.
* **Stormbound Kesh — elite hybrid.** A corrupted or militant glider uses a harpoon tether, short-range chain lightning and aerial repositioning. It is vulnerable immediately after the tether misses.

**Boss — The Tempest Conductor:**

A weather regulator fused with fungal growth and old electromagnetic machinery.

* Phase one activates lightning pylons and direct electrical shots.
* Phase two introduces rotating wind fields, suction and air mines.
* Phase three requires the player to disable the final pylons while the boss moves between floating islands.

The core appears once the electrical network is interrupted in the correct sequence. This is a late-game test of grappling to changing targets, preserving momentum through wind and choosing when to release.

## 6. Encounter Progression and Implementation Notes

Each world should introduce one primary movement pressure and then remix it with mechanics learned earlier.

A basic world progression can be:

1. A melee enemy teaches the world’s primary movement pressure.
2. A ranged enemy forces the player to read cover and altitude.
3. A controller changes the route without removing the grapple system.
4. A support enemy introduces target priority.
5. An elite combines two previously learned pressures.
6. The boss combines the world’s central mechanic with one earlier world mechanic.

The first gameplay slice should use The Last Garden with Rootstalker, Spore Kite, Mycelial Weaver and Garden Heart. This tests melee pursuit, direct and arcing ranged pressure, visibility reduction, sticky movement, temporary obstacle growth, support tethers, grapple-compatible arena reshaping and a boss with both ground and air AOE.

The initial enemy data should remain compatible with the planned architecture. An `EnemyDefinition` should eventually identify:

* role tags;
* preferred distance and altitude;
* allowed movement surfaces;
* attack abilities;
* telegraph duration and active window;
* hit shape or projectile path;
* status and movement effects;
* grapple interaction;
* weak-point or recovery conditions;
* corruption modifiers;
* intended encounter and world usage.

An `AttackAbility` should describe the behavior of the attack separately from its damage values. The ability may specify whether it is melee, close-range, direct ranged, arcing ranged, ground AOE or air AOE; how it affects player velocity; how it modifies surfaces; and how it responds to grappling.

The most important implementation rule is that enemy abilities should usually alter the player's decisions while preserving the movement system. The world is allowed to become dangerous, but it should remain interactive.
