## 1. The Starting World — The First Bloom

The opening world should be a functioning, isolated human settlement rather than a ruin.

The protagonist begins inside the life the Severance refugees built for themselves:

* a small village rather than a city;
* scattered homesteads rather than dense urban neighborhoods;
* practical crafts rather than advanced industry;
* inherited knowledge that is useful but deliberately incomplete;
* a culture that treats the old world as both dangerous and vaguely mythical.

The player should initially understand why this society chose isolation. It is safe, intimate and materially competent. It is also restrictive. The first portal is therefore not discovered by a professional expedition or a chosen hero. It is opened by two young people who are trying to postpone the moment when their lives become settled.

### The Village and the Choosing

The village sits in a sheltered valley near a waterfall, forest and broken highland terrain. The surrounding area has been explored enough for the villagers to know its reliable paths, hunting grounds and dangerous places, but not enough for anyone to have mapped the entire region.

The village is deliberately small. Outlying homes are normal because the founders prohibited large settlements, and because fields, grazing land and workshops need to be spread out. The protagonist's home is the farthest of these homesteads, beyond the main cluster of buildings. It is close enough to reach the village in a short walk, but far enough away that the protagonist has grown up with more independence than most villagers.

At the beginning of the story, both boys' parents are away on a trading journey. They have taken wares to another village some distance away and will be gone for several days. The trip is ordinary and necessary, but their absence leaves the protagonist's house without adult supervision at exactly the wrong time. It also means the boys can treat the home as a private place to investigate their discovery instead of immediately bringing it to the village leader.

At eighteen, every young person must choose a role that the community can depend on. The choice is presented as a coming-of-age freedom, but it is also an obligation. A village with too many hunters and no miller cannot survive, so the village leader and the elders exert pressure when a person's preference does not match the community's needs.

The protagonist is approximately seventeen, with one month remaining before the Choosing. He has not decided what he wants to become. He is capable in the outdoors, knows the local terrain unusually well and is happiest when he is somewhere he is not supposed to be. None of those qualities translate neatly into one of the village's recognized roles.

His closest friend has just turned eighteen.

The friend is curious, mechanically gifted and more intelligent than the village's simple title system has room to acknowledge. He has become the village smith, but the position also makes him its closest equivalent to an engineer. He repairs tools, maintains water and agricultural equipment, experiments with alloys and builds mechanisms that nobody else in the village fully understands.

He originally wanted to explore the continent. The village leader convinced him to stay by framing the choice as a moral responsibility: the village already has enough people who want to discover new places, but it needs someone who can keep the existing place alive. The friend accepts the role, partly because the argument is valid and partly because he feels guilty for wanting to leave.

That decision creates the emotional pressure between the two friends.

They are genuinely close. They have grown up together, explored the same ravines and forest paths, and know each other's habits almost as well as their own. They also compete constantly. They keep informal scores over who reaches a ledge first, who finds the better route and who is right about a dangerous shortcut. The protagonist is usually the bolder explorer. The friend is usually the one who understands how a thing works once they find it.

Each envies the other.

The protagonist sees his friend's new position as a surrender to the village. The friend sees the protagonist's uncertainty as a luxury made possible by other people's work. Neither says this plainly, because their rivalry is also how they express affection.

### The Forbidden Mushroom

Mushrooms are treated as poisonous and socially dangerous throughout the village. Children are taught never to touch unfamiliar growth, and adults burn or isolate any large or unusually colored mushroom they find. The warning is not entirely superstition: ordinary fungal blooms can cause severe illness, and the community has inherited fragments of the founders' fear without understanding its technological origin.

The village has no concept of engineered portal fungus. It only has a prohibition:

> **Do not bring strange mushrooms home.**

The specimen the friends find is dormant and sealed inside an old expedition sack. It looks like a dense, dry, dark growth rather than a living network. It does not resemble the small mushrooms that cause ordinary sickness, which is part of why the friend is fascinated by it. The protagonist is less impressed. He sees a forbidden biological object beside a dead Giant and argues that both should be left where they are.

The specimen is in fact a surviving culture of the old portal fungus. It has been inactive for a century, but it still contains the resonance patterns needed to reconnect with an ancient anchor.

### The Cavern Discovery

One month before the protagonist's birthday, the two friends decide to push beyond the furthest routes they have explored together. The trip is partly an adventure and partly an argument about the future. The friend wants to prove that becoming the village smith has not made him ordinary. The protagonist wants one more expedition before the Choosing can close off his options.

They reach a waterfall and find a narrow route behind it. The passage is not marked on any village map. They begin competing over who can cross a natural shelf first, and the weakened ground gives way beneath them. They fall into a cavern below the waterfall.

The fall is frightening but survivable. More importantly, it leaves them inside a sealed chamber that no living villager has seen.

There they discover the Giant.

The Giant is dead, preserved beneath the cavern's cold spray and mineral deposits. This is not a monster encounter. It is the first physical evidence that the stories about Giants contain a distorted memory of a real people. The Giant's body is large enough to make the friends feel like children in the chamber, but its equipment is clearly manufactured rather than ceremonial.

Beside the body is an expedition pack. Inside they find:

* a folded traversal body suit and harness;
* the dormant fungal specimen;
* unfamiliar tools and fittings;
* the insignia of the original Pathfinder organization.

The friends do not know the words *harness*, *Pathfinder* or *portal*. They interpret the suit as some kind of protective body armor, and the insignia as evidence that the Giant belonged to an organized people rather than being a supernatural creature.

The protagonist's first instinct is to leave everything in the cavern. The dead should be left undisturbed, and taking the mushroom violates the strongest rule they have been taught. The friend argues that they may never find the chamber again. He also points out that if they return to the village with nothing but a story, nobody will believe them.

The argument is strengthened by their rivalry. The friend does not want to return from the greatest discovery of their lives empty-handed, especially now that he has supposedly chosen a life of staying put. Eventually he convinces the protagonist to take the pack.

### Two Secrets

The friends decide not to return to the village with the discovery. The protagonist's parents are away, the friend's parents are away and the nearest adults are far enough away that the boys can investigate without being interrupted. They go directly to the protagonist's house.

They hide the fungal specimen beside the house. They do not understand containment, so they loosely bury the sack under soil and leaves rather than opening it or destroying it. They know mushrooms are considered toxic, but the discovery is too interesting to throw away. Their decision should feel like the kind of foolish compromise made by intelligent teenagers: cautious enough to believe they are being responsible, reckless enough to create a catastrophe.

They bring the body suit inside and take it to the protagonist's bedroom. The friend has only the portable tools he carries for field repairs and whatever household tools he can find, but his curiosity is stronger than the limitations of the workspace.

He spends the evening examining the suit. He tests clasps, flexible plates, lenses, cables and unfamiliar contact points. The suit is too sophisticated to be armor, but too tactile and mechanical to resemble anything from the village. It has no obvious source of power and no visible weapon.

The protagonist eventually puts it on so they can test how it moves on a human body. It fits more naturally than either of them expects. The friend forms a theory about how to activate it, probably by closing a circuit through one of the hand controls or pulling a lever near the wrist. He tries the sequence.

Nothing happens.

They leave the suit on the protagonist because removing it risks damaging the unfamiliar fastenings, and because they are exhausted from the expedition. They fall asleep in the bedroom with the protagonist still wearing the harness. The friend's field tools, the sack's empty packing material and the unfamiliar suit are all gathered around them, turning the bedroom into a secret workshop for one night.

This is the last moment when the discovery still feels like a secret they can put back.

### The First Bloom

In the early morning, the two boys wake in the bedroom as the house shakes. At first they think the cavern may have collapsed or that the earthquake is a local aftershock. Then the floor tilts beneath them and a deep cracking sound comes from the main part of the house.

They get outside and see something rising above the trees. A colossal mushroom is growing from the ground beneath the protagonist's home. Its stalk has seized the central part of the house—the kitchen, living room and adjoining common spaces—and is lifting it into the sky. The bedroom where the boys slept is left behind at the edge of the structure, cracked but still standing. The rest of the home is being carried upward in pieces.

The arrangement should make the bloom immediately personal and visually legible: the boys are safe for the moment because their bedroom has been spared, while the rooms that represent family life are disappearing above them. The kitchen hearth, living-room table, shelves and roof beams become parts of the rising route.

The village reacts with panic. The old stories say that a large mushroom brings sickness, but no story describes one that grows faster than a person can run. Adults begin shouting for people to move away from the homestead. The village leader tries to organize an evacuation while others reach for fire, axes and water.

The protagonist does not wait for instructions. He runs toward the bloom because the main part of his home is inside it, still wearing the body suit.

The friend follows from behind.

The suit remains inert during the first part of the run. As the protagonist approaches the mushroom, small lines and fittings along the harness begin to emit a weak light. The friend recognizes that the suit is responding to the bloom and shouts for him to activate it.

This time the activation works.

The first grapple fires from the harness and catches on the mushroom's living surface. The protagonist is pulled off the ground before he understands what has happened. He nearly loses his grip, slams into the stalk and discovers that the suit is not a climbing device in the ordinary sense. It is a traversal system that converts a target into a route.

The friend is frightened by how quickly the protagonist is being pulled upward. He also recognizes the impossible opportunity in front of them. The mushroom is responding to the old equipment, the house is being carried toward its upper growth and the path cannot remain stable forever.

Their old pattern returns under pressure: the friend is the one who understands the mechanism, and the protagonist is the one willing to use it.

The friend tells the protagonist to go. He says he will catch up as quickly as he can, whether by finding another route, retrieving tools or helping the villagers below. The promise is partly reassurance and partly a challenge. Neither friend is ready to admit that the first crossing may separate them.

### The House in the Sky

The ascent becomes the opening traversal sequence.

The protagonist grapples from fungal ridges to broken beams, wall-runs along sections of his own house and uses familiar objects in unfamiliar positions. A kitchen window becomes a platform. A roof becomes a vertical wall. The family table, shelves and hearth appear at impossible heights along the stalk, allowing the player to see that the bloom is not merely destroying the house; it is pulling the communal heart of the home into itself.

The largest section of the house rests at the top of the mushroom. It is the main living and kitchen area, torn upward almost intact. Inside it, the protagonist finds a space that is larger than the room should be. The walls have opened around a circular structure of fungal tissue, metal and pale light. The dormant anchor beneath the homestead has been reconnected to the specimen, and the old portal system has grown a new path around the house.

There is a portal inside.

The protagonist has no historical framework for what he is seeing. To him it is a doorway made from the forbidden thing his village fears, connected to the dead Giant's impossible equipment. The friend is still climbing below, promising to catch up. The village is below him. His home is above him. The only recognizable route leads through the light.

The protagonist crosses first, believing the friend will follow immediately. The friend reaches the top moments later, just in time to see the protagonist on the other side. For a brief interval they can still see and hear each other through the portal, but the opening begins to contract as the bloom consumes the last of its resonance. Neither can cross back in time.

The starting world ends with the portal disappearing between them—not with the discovery of the doorway, but with the first connection becoming the first separation.

### Why the Bloom Happens

The recommended explanation is that the opening is caused by three pieces of the old system meeting again:

1. **The fungal specimen** is a dormant portal culture carried by the Giant.
2. **The harness** is a Pathfinder traversal and resonance device. The friend's attempted activation places it into a low-power standby state even though it does not yet produce a grapple.
3. **The protagonist's homestead** was built over a buried, inert portal anchor. The founders dismantled the active machinery, but one anchor was too dangerous to remove and was hidden beneath an outlying home. Its purpose was forgotten over generations.

The friend's tinkering wakes the harness. The buried specimen responds through the anchor during the night. At dawn, the fungus blooms upward, using the house and the old anchor as a physical and energetic scaffold. Because the anchor sits beneath the central foundation, the mushroom takes the kitchen and living room first while leaving the bedroom wing behind. The suit only fully activates when it comes close enough to the living growth to complete the resonance.

This keeps the event from feeling random while preserving the characters' point of view: they caused it, but they did not understand what they were activating.

### What the Starting World Establishes

The opening world should establish the game's central tensions on a personal scale:

* **Duty versus freedom:** the friend stays to serve the village while the protagonist is about to choose whether to stay at all.
* **Curiosity versus caution:** the friend keeps touching things that should have been left alone, while the protagonist recognizes the danger but is pulled into it anyway.
* **Connection versus isolation:** the village's rules protected it for a century, but those same rules left its people unable to understand the technology now changing their lives.
* **History versus myth:** the Giant is real, the Pathfinder insignia is real and the old portal still works, even though the village's explanation for all three has become legend.
* **Friendship versus rivalry:** the first portal opens because the friends act together, but the ascent begins with one friend going ahead and the other promising to follow.

The mushroom should not be presented as simply evil. It is dangerous, invasive and capable of catastrophe, but it is also the surviving mechanism that reconnects the protagonist to the wider history of the worlds. The starting world should leave the player with the first version of the question that later worlds complicate:

> **Is a dangerous connection still worth making if isolation is the only alternative you have ever known?**

### Details to Keep Flexible for Now

Several choices can remain open while the later worlds are developed:

* the names of the protagonist, friend, village and village leader;
* what wares the parents are carrying and which distant village they are visiting;
* whether the founders deliberately placed a homestead over the inert anchor or whether later settlers did so unknowingly;
* whether the Giant carried the specimen as an emergency means of reopening the old route or simply as Pathfinder research that survived the war;
* the exact form of the friend's beacon and the point in the Human Homeworld arc where the reunion occurs.

The emotional sequence should remain fixed even if those surface details change:

> **A final adventure before adulthood → a forbidden discovery → a secret shared between friends → an accidental bloom → a house lifted into the sky → a first step through history → the portal closes between them.**

## 2. The Friend's Parallel Arc — The Beacon

The protagonist's journey should not begin when the friend disappears. The friend becomes the second half of the opening story the moment the portal closes.

The protagonist crosses into the unknown believing that the friend will follow immediately. The friend remains in the starting world believing that the protagonist is still reachable. Both spend the next several months acting on the same promise:

> **Find a way back to each other.**

Their methods eventually lead them toward opposite sides of the game's central conflict.

### The Separation

The protagonist enters the portal first. The friend reaches the top of the mushroom only moments later, still expecting to step through behind him.

For a brief time, the portal remains open as a shrinking window. The protagonist turns around and sees his friend on the other side. The friend sees the alien world behind the protagonist. Both try to cross or reach through, but the portal is already contracting.

The protagonist may fire the grapple toward the friend, but the harness cannot attach across the unstable boundary. The friend reaches for him, but the portal is no longer a doorway that can support another crossing. It is a connection consuming the last of its available resonance.

They watch each other in horror as the opening closes.

This should not feel like the protagonist knowingly chooses exploration over his friend. He enters because the friend tells him to go, because both believe the friend will follow and because neither understands that the bloom can only sustain one crossing. The friend is not too late because he hesitates; he is too late because the rules of the old technology are unknown to both of them.

The disappearance gives the friend a clear wound and a clear objective. The protagonist is not merely lost in another world. He is alive, visible and suddenly unreachable.

### The Eradication

The village arrives shortly afterward.

The villagers see a towering toxic mushroom wrapped around the protagonist's home. They do not understand that the portal has already closed, and they have no reason to believe the protagonist survived. To them, the bloom is an uncontrolled outbreak and a direct threat to the settlement.

They decide to eradicate it.

The response should be frightened and practical rather than cruel. The village leader orders the growth burned, cut and isolated before it can spread. The villagers dismantle the parts of the house that remain caught in the stalk and destroy the visible fungal tissue.

The friend knows that destroying the bloom may destroy the only evidence of where the protagonist went. He cannot openly stop the village without revealing that he helped cause the event and hid the specimen. Instead, he secretly salvages a small collection of pieces before the main growth is destroyed.

This is the second separation. The first takes the protagonist away. The second threatens to remove the only remaining path to him.

### Studying Without Planting

The friend keeps the fragments hidden and studies them over the following months. He does not plant them.

That distinction should remain central to his character. He has seen what an uncontrolled bloom can do to a home and a village. He understands that simply growing another mushroom would be reckless, so he keeps the samples sealed in containers and observes how they respond to heat, vibration, light, metal and carefully controlled electrical current.

His new role as the village smith gives him access to tools, metalworking equipment and a reason to work late without immediately attracting suspicion. The same position that once felt like a compromise becomes the foundation of his research.

He gradually discovers that the samples are not only alive. They are receiving and repeating patterns. Some patterns cause the tissue to become active. Others produce a weak glow or a change in growth direction. The friend does not yet understand the purpose, but he realizes that the fungus is communicating with something beyond the village.

This period should run in parallel with the protagonist's early exploration. While the protagonist learns to use the harness in unfamiliar worlds, the friend learns to read the biological system that powers the old network.

### The Beacon

The friend eventually builds a device that does not grow a portal but signals one.

Using the surviving fungal fragments, old metal from the bloom and his own mechanical experiments, he creates a crude resonance beacon. It is an analog machine of coils, plates, tuned components and living material held in strict containment.

The first versions fail. Some go dark. Some produce only heat. One may briefly cause a small fungal tendril to seek a direction before going inert. The friend keeps refining the device rather than planting the samples, because he now understands that the signal matters more than the growth.

Over months, the beacon begins broadcasting a recognizable Pathfinder pattern.

It is not a message in language. It is a location signal—a lighthouse built from the remains of the first bloom. The friend is telling the surviving human network:

> **This world is still here. Something from the old system is still alive.**

The signal is weak and intermittent, which gives the human Continuity network time to detect it, interpret it and prepare a response. The reconnection is therefore deliberate and technically difficult, not an immediate consequence of the protagonist's portal.

### The Human Expedition

The original human civilization eventually triggers a portal to the friend's world.

The expedition arrives expecting to find a lost settlement, an old portal anchor or a surviving human colony. Instead, they find an isolated village, a damaged bloom and the young smith who created the signal.

The friend goes with them when they offer access to human archives, advanced equipment and the surviving portal infrastructure. He is not abandoning the village. He believes that reaching the human world is the only realistic way to find the protagonist.

He becomes the expedition's local technical expert. He can explain what happened at the bloom, how the beacon works and what little he knows about the harness. The humans can provide the knowledge he lacks, while he provides the living specimen and the coordinates they could not have recovered alone.

The friend continues working with human scientists to study the fungal system and identify the route the protagonist took. His research is driven by a personal goal, but it also gives the human civilization something it desperately needs: a way to restore reliable access to the wider network.

### Why He Supports the Humans

The friend arrives in the human world expecting to find answers. He also finds a civilization in permanent resource conflict.

The human homeworld is beautiful and populated by ordinary people, but its cities, hospitals and agricultural systems are under constant pressure. Shortages lead to political conflict and sometimes violence. The friend sees that the resource crisis is not an abstract justification invented by leaders. It is harming people he can meet.

This changes his position.

He does not initially support uncontrolled expansion. His preferred solution is a limited and regulated reconnection:

* controlled portals;
* strict biological containment;
* small exploratory teams;
* no automatic extraction rights;
* research before large-scale transfer.

But he believes connection must remain possible. He has experienced isolation as the loss of his closest friend, and he now sees the human world experiencing isolation as hunger, instability and conflict.

His argument becomes a practical form of Continuity:

> **The portals are dangerous, but closing them permanently leaves people with no way to solve the crisis that is already killing them.**

The human leaders use his qualifications and personal story to support a broader policy than he intended. His careful statements about controlled reconnection become public claims that portal travel is safe again.

They also use the protagonist as erroneous proof. A living traveler from a supposedly lost world is presented as evidence that the old dangers have been overcome. The truth is narrower: one person survived one unusual crossing through one unstable bloom. That does not prove that every world is safe, that every fungal strain is controllable or that extraction can begin again.

The friend understands some of these limitations, but his need for the project to succeed makes him underestimate how quickly safeguards can become obstacles in the eyes of people facing immediate shortages.

### The Late Reunion

By the time the protagonist reaches the Human Homeworld, the original purpose of his journey has changed.

At the beginning, he wants to find his friend and return home. After traveling through the other worlds, he learns what the portal network did to civilizations and sees how easily a necessary connection becomes entitlement, extraction and war. His goal becomes preventing history from repeating, even if that means accepting that he may never see his friend again.

The friend, meanwhile, has spent those same months doing everything he can to find the protagonist. He has created the beacon, crossed into the human world and helped rebuild the path that eventually brings the protagonist there.

The reunion should begin with genuine relief. Neither expected the other to survive. For a moment, the separation is undone.

Then the contradiction becomes unavoidable.

The friend is now part of the human reconnection effort. The protagonist has arrived as someone increasingly suspicious of that effort. The friend believes he has fulfilled the promise to find him. The protagonist sees that the path created to find one person is becoming a justification for opening every world again.

Their conflict is not a simple betrayal. Both have remained loyal to the promise they made at the first portal, but they have spent months learning different lessons from the separation.

The friend believes:

> **Connection is worth the danger because people cannot survive isolation forever.**

The protagonist believes:

> **Connection cannot be justified merely because people are afraid of what isolation costs.**

The friend becomes the personal face of Continuity for the protagonist. He is not a distant administrator or an abstract political faction. He is the person the protagonist crossed worlds to find.

The original goal has not truly become irrelevant. The protagonist has buried it beneath a larger responsibility. The reunion forces him to confront the fact that his personal hope and the political crisis are still connected.

### Narrative Function

The two arcs should remain different in form:

* the protagonist moves outward through worlds and learns the consequences of the old network;
* the friend moves inward toward the surviving human civilization and learns why the network was considered necessary;
* the protagonist becomes more cautious about reconnection;
* the friend becomes more committed to making reconnection work;
* the protagonist is separated from home by a portal that closes;
* the friend helps reopen the network because he refuses to accept that separation as permanent.

Their reunion is therefore the moment when the game's personal story and political story become the same story.

The friend should not be treated as a hidden villain. His position is strongest when he is partly right. Controlled connection may genuinely be necessary to prevent human collapse. The tragedy is that his success gives leaders the confidence to expand faster than the safeguards can support.

The friend should leave the protagonist—and the player—with the uncomfortable version of the Continuity argument:

> **You learned what connection did to the worlds. I learned what the absence of connection did to us.**

## 3. The Human Homeworld

This should be one of the game's largest reversals.

After everything the protagonist has learned, they finally reach the civilization responsible for much of the catastrophe.

And it is...

**beautiful.**

Children play.

Universities operate.

Hospitals work.

People make art.

Families live ordinary lives.

They aren't monsters.

Most citizens know almost nothing about what happened.

Then the protagonist appears.

A human from a world thought lost.

With functioning portal technology.

By this point, the friend has already been living in the human world for months. His beacon brought the starting world back to the attention of the surviving human network, and he has worked with human scientists to understand the fungal system and search for the protagonist's route.

He has become a respected technical voice in the reconnection effort. He argues for controlled portals and careful study, but his qualifications and personal story are used to support a much broader policy than he intended.

The protagonist becomes erroneous proof that the age of isolation can end safely. His survival demonstrates that one person crossed one unstable portal. Continuity leaders present it as evidence that the old dangers have been overcome.

The friend is on the human side of the argument when the protagonist arrives. He believes the portals must remain available because the human homeworld is locked in constant conflict over dwindling resources. The reunion therefore becomes both a personal recovery and an ideological confrontation.

The political slogan practically writes itself:

> **THE WORLDS ARE OPEN AGAIN.**

And because the protagonist has traveled through numerous apparently viable worlds, Continuity leaders see an opportunity to finally solve the century-long resource crisis.

Exploration restarts.

Then extraction.

Then resistance.

Then casualties.

Then propaganda.

And eventually the protagonist recognizes exactly what is happening:

**the history they've spent the entire game uncovering is beginning again around them.**

Some of the leaders who restarted it realize the same thing and attempt to stop it.

But now they discover the final expression of your theme:

> **People can create systems that eventually become larger than their intentions.**

They built the propaganda.

They promised prosperity.

They told people the other worlds would save them.

They celebrated the explorers.

Now saying *stop* doesn't undo any of that.

## 4. The Three-Act Story and Mission Structure

The story should be built as a three-act progression. Each act asks a larger version of the same question about connection:

* **Act 1 — The Unstable Door:** Can the protagonist understand the worlds and find his friend?
* **Act 2 — The Controlled Door:** Can the portal network be used responsibly?
* **Act 3 — The Door That Must Be Chosen:** What should be done when control fails?

The acts should share one primary story path. Major decisions should change relationships, survivors, enemy states, mission conditions and the meaning of the ending rather than creating three entirely separate campaigns.

### Act 1 — The Unstable Door

The starting-world story and the friend's parallel beacon arc form the first act.

The protagonist begins as a seventeen-year-old who is one month away from the village's required role Choosing. He discovers the Giant, the fungal specimen and the traversal harness with his best friend, accidentally activates the first bloom and crosses through the portal alone.

#### Unstable portal travel

Natural or accidentally regenerated portals are not reliable doorways. They can open long enough for a person or a small amount of matter to pass through, but the connection destabilizes after matter crosses.

The protagonist cannot simply step back through the same portal. The passage loses its phase lock, collapses or redirects its remaining energy into another fungal growth. The harness helps him identify and activate available connections, but it does not stabilize the portal behind him.

This gives the protagonist's Act 1 world hopping a clear purpose. He is moving forward because every crossing removes the route behind him. He is trying to understand the portal system and find a way back to his friend, while each new world reveals another part of the history of fungal energy, the Giants and the war.

The worlds he visits should also give him the first encounters with:

* corrupted Giants who appear to confirm the human belief that Giants are dangerous;
* wary but uncorrupted Giants who can be avoided, helped or cautiously approached by a lone human;
* communities that survived earlier fungal releases;
* the consequences of Severance, Continuity and Concord before the protagonist knows those philosophies by name.

A lone human is less threatening than an organized expedition. The protagonist can often get around Giant groups by avoiding them, helping an individual or proving that he is not establishing an outpost. This becomes much more difficult in Act 2 when humans arrive with equipment, quotas and a visible military or industrial presence.

#### The friend's Act 1 path

After watching the protagonist disappear, the friend remains behind. The village attempts to eradicate the bloom, and he secretly preserves small samples without planting them. He studies their reactions and eventually builds a contained resonance beacon.

The beacon does not grow a portal. It broadcasts a recognizable Pathfinder signal that allows the surviving human network to detect the starting world. The original human civilization opens a portal to the friend's world, and he travels with the expedition to the Human Homeworld.

There he learns how to combine the fungal system with surviving human electromagnetic stabilization technology. The friend believes that controlled portals can allow people to reconnect without repeating the uncontrolled spread that destroyed the old worlds.

#### Act 1 endpoint

The protagonist reaches the Human Homeworld and reunites with the friend.

The protagonist has achieved his original personal goal, but the journey has changed it. He no longer wants only to return home. He now understands that his friend is connected to a much larger question: whether the worlds should be connected again at all.

### Act 2 — The Controlled Door

#### Reconnection and the expedition

After the reunion, the friend convinces the protagonist to join the new human expedition program.

The expedition is presented as a controlled alternative to the reckless expansion of the past. The protagonist becomes a field and traversal specialist. The friend is responsible for portal calibration, stabilization and interpreting the fungal system.

The early missions should succeed. The expeditioners restore supply routes, recover materials, provide aid and rescue people without immediately causing a catastrophe. The player should understand why ordinary humans support the program.

The friend can honestly argue:

> **We finally have a chance to do this correctly.**

#### The expedition mission loop

Each expedition should have at least two goals:

1. A practical objective such as rescuing people, recovering resources or stabilizing an anchor.
2. A local relationship or research objective involving the people and fungal life of that world.

The missions should produce different kinds of progress:

* **Portal materials** such as conductors, anchors and field components improve future stabilization.
* **Biological samples** reveal how different fungal strains interact with different hosts.
* **Collaboration** gives the protagonists the cultural knowledge, trust and consent needed to apply that research safely.

The expedition can sometimes obtain one form of progress without the others. Taking a sample by force may improve the raw research while damaging collaboration. Helping a community may improve trust without producing a useful biological sample. The strongest outcomes require both.

#### The gradual compromise

The human leadership's greed should appear as a series of successful compromises:

* portal windows are shortened to increase the number of expeditions;
* quotas are raised after earlier missions exceed expectations;
* quarantine periods are reduced;
* native warnings are dismissed because previous warnings proved overly cautious;
* stabilizers are reused beyond their recommended maintenance limits;
* richer resource zones are opened before the full safety survey is complete.

None of these decisions needs to fail immediately. Their success teaches the characters and the player that the restrictions may be excessive.

The final catastrophe should be the result of a chain rather than one obviously evil action:

> **Resource pressure → increased quotas → weakened safeguards → normalized risk → personal emergency → one final shortcut.**

#### Giants during Act 2

The expedition encounters Giants throughout the controlled-expedition phase.

Some Giants are corrupted by the fungus and become enemies. Some are uncorrupted survivor groups who are frightened, defensive or openly hostile toward human equipment. All Giants should be wary of humans, even when they are willing to cooperate.

The official human civilization has taught that Giants are evil. Corrupted Giants make that story appear credible at first. Survivor groups challenge it.

Possible Giant missions include:

* fighting a corrupted Giant group that is attacking an expedition route;
* rescuing an injured survivor group that refuses to let humans approach;
* supplying a Giant community while abandoning an extraction objective;
* helping a Giant group escape a fungal bloom;
* deciding whether a corrupted Giant can be saved or must be fought.

The protagonist can often approach Giant groups more successfully when he is alone. A full human expedition looks much more like an invasion force. The team's actions determine whether a survivor group accepts aid, remains cautious or refuses all further contact.

At least one Giant group should receive meaningful aid during the story. This is a required demonstration that Humans and Giants can work together again. Other groups can have variable outcomes based on the player's decisions.

A saved Giant group may later provide:

* a safe route through Giant territory;
* knowledge about an old portal anchor;
* structural or geological expertise;
* a biological sample or symbiosis record;
* a cautious alliance during the Act 3 war.

A group that is frightened or mistreated may refuse this assistance. Cooperation should be earned and remain conditional rather than turning into instant friendship.

#### The Resource World

The resource-rich hostile world should be a place the protagonist has already visited during Act 1.

During the earlier visit, he meets a refugee community that has already rebuilt its life there after escaping a fungal release on another world. The protagonist knows the world as a home, not as an empty resource site. The unstable portal forces him to leave before he can remain or promise meaningful help.

In Act 2, the stabilized expedition returns to the same world. Human records classify it as unclaimed, underused or abandoned, but the protagonist recognizes the people living there.

This makes the mission immediately personal:

* Act 1 presents the world as a refuge;
* Act 2 presents it as a human resource opportunity;
* the protagonist knows that the resource site is someone else's home;
* the friend has to decide whether controlled extraction is acceptable when its consequences are visible.

The human expeditioner who eventually causes the release should be someone the protagonist trusts. Their family on the Human Homeworld is facing an immediate crisis, such as failing medical equipment, a collapsing hospital or severe resource shortages. The expedition's quota may directly affect whether their family receives help.

The expeditioner asks to access a sealed high-yield deposit and is refused because it lies outside the stabilized route or near dormant fungal growth. They take the risk anyway, believing that a single shortcut can save their family.

The deposit's energy and biological conditions allow the engineered fungus to grow explosively. The release overwhelms the stabilization system, spreads through the world and begins destabilizing the wider portal network.

The refugees the protagonist met in Act 1 are caught in the destruction. The expeditioner who caused the release becomes corrupted, making the disaster both a political failure and a personal tragedy.

#### Act 2 endpoint

The portal network collapses while the expedition is trying to evacuate.

The protagonist and friend are caught in a portal together and land on a Concord world. The rest of the expedition team is left behind on the hostile resource world.

The friend immediately argues that they must reopen the portal to save their friends. The protagonist must decide whether to act immediately, refuse the connection or delay until they can create a stable route.

### Act 3 — The Door That Must Be Chosen

#### The Concord refuge

The Concord world has achieved a successful fungal symbiosis. Its inhabitants are healthy and their society functions through a peaceful relationship with the local fungal network.

However, they cannot create stable portals. They either lost the required technology during the war or deliberately rejected it. The protagonists therefore need to combine Concord biological knowledge with human stabilization technology.

The first half of Act 3 is about gathering the materials and knowledge needed to stabilize a portal from the Concord world.

#### The immediate rescue decision

The friend's position is emotionally understandable. The expeditioners are trapped, and he has already lost the protagonist once. He does not want another separation to become permanent.

The protagonist's response creates different immediate conditions without creating a completely separate campaign.

##### Severance response

The protagonist convinces the friend not to reopen the damaged portal because the connection could infect the Concord world.

The hostile resource world is not revisited. The expeditioners are presumed lost, and the protagonists travel directly to another known expedition world once they can create a stable route.

The Concord world avoids the immediate infection, but the protagonists must accept that they abandoned people who might have been saved.

##### Continuity response

The protagonist agrees to an emergency opening.

The portal reveals that the landing area on the hostile world has already been destroyed. Before the opening collapses, a mushroom-infected creature crosses into the Concord world and escapes.

This emergency connection is not stable enough for a rescue. The protagonists still need to complete the portal-building missions, but their work becomes increasingly difficult as the infected creature spreads through local wildlife and fungal growth.

The hostile resource world can be revisited later. By then, only one surviving expeditioner may remain, and that person is corrupted by the mushroom.

##### Concord response

The protagonist refuses to open the portal immediately but promises to return through a stable connection.

The protagonists gather materials and work with the Concord society before opening the route. They later return to the hostile resource world and find two stranded expeditioners:

* the expeditioner who caused the disaster, now corrupted;
* another expeditioner who is still alive and can be rescued.

This demonstrates that delay can be an active form of care rather than simple abandonment.

#### The first stable Act 3 portal

Once the protagonists can stabilize their own portal, they can choose whether to return to the hostile resource world or travel directly to another world previously visited by the expedition that contains a human presence.

The hostile-world route provides additional rescue, evidence and consequences depending on the earlier decision. The direct route preserves the Severance decision and moves immediately toward the wider crisis.

Regardless of route, the protagonists eventually arrive at a known expedition world with human settlements, equipment and portal infrastructure.

There they discover that unstable portals have appeared across multiple worlds. The outbreak is no longer isolated to the resource world.

#### The War of Open Doors

The portal outbreak creates a new war between Humans and Giants.

The conflict should not represent every Human fighting every Giant. It should contain several overlapping conflicts:

* human expeditioners defend supply routes and resource stations;
* human leaders try to preserve access to the worlds;
* Giant groups defend their own worlds and the small civilizations living among them;
* some Giants attack human facilities because they see another invasion beginning;
* some humans protect settlements rather than extract resources;
* corrupted Giants and infected creatures attack everyone;
* small civilizations are trapped between forces they did not choose.

The Giants have a small number of worlds containing their own settlements and dependent civilizations. The protagonists should see Giants protecting these communities, not simply defending territory for its own sake.

The war also gives the saved Giant group from Act 2 a narrative payoff. If the protagonists earned its trust, the group may help them move through Giant territory, negotiate with other communities or defend a portal stabilization site. Other Giant groups may remain hostile or refuse aid.

#### The Concord world as home base

The Concord world becomes the protagonists' base for the second half of Act 3.

It provides a stable place where they can:

* return between missions;
* consult Concord fungal experts;
* study biological samples;
* build and repair stabilization equipment;
* meet human, Giant and other species representatives;
* decide which portal crisis to address next.

The friend handles the technical side of stabilization and research. The protagonist handles field missions, local relationships, rescues and dangerous anchor sites.

The resulting gameplay loop is:

> **Return to the Concord base → choose a portal crisis → stabilize an anchor or rescue a community → gather materials, samples and knowledge → return with new options.**

#### Three forms of mission progress

Each Act 3 mission should provide one or more of three progress types.

##### Portal materials

The protagonists recover conductors, anchors, regulators and other components needed to stabilize the known portal network.

##### Biological samples

The protagonists study fungal strains, infected creatures, compatible hosts and symbiotic networks. Samples should be obtained through observation, voluntary contribution or carefully controlled recovery whenever possible.

Corrupted organisms may offer valuable information, but studying them should carry ethical and biological risks. A sample taken by force may provide data while damaging the relationship with that species.

##### Collaboration

Collaboration is more than a reputation score. It represents whether different communities are willing to share knowledge, provide consent, participate in experiments and help maintain the network.

Collaboration can be improved by:

* helping a community before asking for its resources;
* allowing local experts to set conditions;
* saving Giants rather than treating them as obstacles;
* sharing human technical knowledge;
* rescuing people even when it delays a quota;
* refusing to use sentient organisms as disposable tools;
* returning to communities and honoring earlier promises.

The strongest symbiosis research requires both biological information and collaboration. A stolen sample may reveal what a fungus can do, but cooperation reveals how it can be used without coercion.

#### Research and stabilization

The friend and the Concord researchers develop the compatibility model, while the protagonists gather portal materials, biological knowledge and collaboration through field missions. The detailed biological rules and network-stabilization missions are defined in [PORTAL_AND_FUNGAL_ECOLOGY.md](PORTAL_AND_FUNGAL_ECOLOGY.md); the mission objectives and encounter conditions are defined in [LEVEL_AND_ENCOUNTER_DESIGN.md](LEVEL_AND_ENCOUNTER_DESIGN.md).

#### The final use of the technology

The protagonists eventually find a way to stabilize the portal outbreak. The solution combines Human engineering, Giant structural knowledge, Concord symbiosis and information from other species.

This solves the immediate crisis but creates the final decision.

##### Continuity

The protagonists give the stabilization technology to the Humans.

The technology saves the Human Homeworld and keeps the portal network stable for the immediate future. Hospitals receive power, supply routes reopen and the human civilization avoids collapse.

However, the protagonists understand what will follow. Stable portals do not prevent:

* resource extraction;
* military occupation;
* political pressure;
* damage to other worlds;
* exploitation of small civilizations;
* the gradual weakening of safety restrictions.

The Humans will continue extracting from other worlds because their survival depends on it. The technology works, but it only buys time before the cycle begins again.

This is a tragic Continuity ending:

> **The protagonists save humanity today while knowingly enabling future harm.**

##### Severance

The protagonists use the stabilization knowledge to close or destroy the remaining portal anchors instead of giving the technology to the Humans.

The war ends because the routes disappear. Worlds become isolated again, and further exploitation is prevented. But human settlements lose access to resources, medicine and rescue routes, while stranded populations may be left behind.

Severance prevents another interworld catastrophe by accepting the suffering caused by permanent separation.

##### Concord

The protagonists combine stabilization technology with the Concord fungal network and distribute control between the connected worlds.

The resulting system can support voluntary, locally governed connections. It may allow more species to survive symbiosis, but it is not a perfect cure. Some hosts may die, some communities may reject it and some species may be biologically incompatible.

The quality of this ending depends on collaboration:

* low collaboration produces a fragile network that supports only a few species;
* moderate collaboration produces limited coexistence with serious risks;
* high collaboration allows Humans, Giants and other species to participate through consent-based agreements.

Concord is not the absence of danger. It is an attempt to make connection something that cannot exist without cooperation.

#### Production-friendly branching

The major story locations can remain shared:

1. the Human Homeworld;
2. the Act 2 expedition worlds;
3. the hostile resource world;
4. the Concord home base;
5. the human-presence world where the war is discovered;
6. the major portal anchors;
7. the final network nexus.

The branches can change:

* whether the hostile world is revisited;
* whether an infected creature reaches the Concord world;
* whether zero, one or two expeditioners survive;
* which Giant groups trust the protagonists;
* which species contribute research;
* which routes are infected or safe;
* how difficult the portal missions become;
* what the friend believes by the final decision;
* how many species can participate in the Concord outcome.

The most useful persistent states are:

* **Portal stability:** how much of the known network is under control;
* **Infection state:** no infection, local infection or spreading infection;
* **Expedition survivors:** the number and condition of the stranded team;
* **Giant collaboration:** hostile, cautious, allied or actively assisting;
* **Cross-species collaboration:** the quality of the shared research and consent process;
* **Friend's conviction:** Continuity, doubt or Concord-oriented cooperation.

The protagonist and friend should still reach the same broad final crisis in every playthrough. The player's choices determine who is present, who trusts them, what the protagonists know and what each final option costs.

The central design principle is:

> **Machines can stabilize portals. Only collaboration can stabilize relationships.**

## 5. The Overall Narrative Shape

This gives the game a strong three-act escalation.

### Act 1 — Separation and Discovery

**Home World**

*What is beyond the clouds?*

↓

**Early Worlds**

*What happened here?*

↓

**Survivor and Faction Worlds**

*What is the fungus, and why did everyone make these choices?*

↓

**Giant World**

*Who were the Humans and Giants before the war?*

↓

**Concord and Continuity Worlds**

*Could connection have worked, and why didn't humanity stop?*

↓

**Human Homeworld**

*They're still alive.*

The protagonist and friend reunite at the end of Act 1.

### Act 2 — Controlled Reconnection

**The Human Expedition**

*Can the portals be used responsibly?*

↓

**Giant Encounters and Alliances**

*Can Humans and Giants work together again?*

↓

**The Resource World**

*What happens when a refuge is treated as an untapped resource?*

↓

**The Catastrophic Release**

*What happens when controlled systems are pushed beyond their limits?*

The protagonist and friend are caught in a collapsing portal and land on the Concord world.

### Act 3 — The War of Open Doors

**The Concord Refuge**

*How can a portal be stabilized without repeating the disaster?*

↓

**The Immediate Rescue Decision**

*Do we reopen the unstable route now, refuse it or wait until it is safe?*

↓

**The Human-Presence World**

*What happens when unstable portals create a war between Humans and Giants?*

↓

**The Concord Home Base**

*Can different species collaborate closely enough to stabilize the known network?*

↓

**The Final Use of the Technology**

**Continuity:** Preserve connection and accept its dangers.

**Concord:** Attempt transformation and coexistence despite enormous immediate risk.

**Severance:** Permanently isolate the worlds and accept the resulting deaths.

And by the time the player chooses, they should have **personally known good people who would support every one of those options**.

That's the condition I'd use to judge whether the worldbuilding is successfully expressing the core thesis. The player shouldn't be asking, *"Which faction was actually the good one?"* They should be thinking, *"I understand exactly why each of them believed they were right—and I've seen what each belief can do to people."*

## 6. World Sequence and Narrative Dependencies

The detailed setting, characters, terrain and encounter rosters for each world are maintained in [WORLD_ATLAS.md](WORLD_ATLAS.md). The narrative sequence is:

| World | Narrative function |
|---|---|
| World 1 — The Last Garden | Introduces the uncertainty of adaptation and the logic of Severance. |
| World 2 — The Hero of the Wall | Shows Continuity as protection that becomes refusal to let go. |
| World 3 — The Severed World | Makes one separation simultaneously salvation and catastrophe. |
| World 4 — The Giant's Memory | Reconnects the protagonist with the Human-Giant past. |
| World 5 — The Joined | Presents Concord as a functioning society with real costs to participation. |
| World 6 — The Gentle Mind | Tests whether understanding can become ownership. |
| World 7 — The Lifeline | Makes resource extraction personal rather than abstract. |
| World 8 — The Crown of Branches | Expands the vertical traversal vocabulary through a naturally aerial civilization. |
| World 9 — The Blue Silence | Explores isolation, memory and preserving routes through a frozen environment. |
| World 10 — The Thunder Shelf | Tests aerial vectors, engineered environments and reconnection at late-game scale. |

World-specific lore and mechanics should be authored together in the atlas, while the protagonist's changing interpretation of each world remains here.
